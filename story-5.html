The Sickroom

Morning light lies across the sickroom threshold. Down the hall wardens pass soft-footed with linen, and somewhere below, Pell's House goes about the work of a house that feeds people.

Round 85. "A day or two." Bria held onto that. Her eyes shone, and she looked down at her own hands, fourteen, still fourteen, only days older instead of the years she had been afraid of, and breathed out a laugh that was mostly relief. "A day or two. I can have a day or two. That's nothing. That's a weekend." Nib told her the rest gently: the crying man might have been real, a fragment brushing hers as the held years ran home, not hers to keep and fading by afternoon. He told her that he had woken without his own memory once, so if something felt missing, her mother's face, say, she should give it a few hours and tell Ansa. "You woke without - " Bria stared at him, this soft-spoken stranger with ink on his fingers, and then at Ansa, who nodded once: true, as far as the House knew. "And you got it back?" She did not wait for the answer. The tiefling at the threshold spoke, horns in the morning light, posture made small, hands where everyone could see them. "I'm Ashen. Door's quiet. Take the water, take the morning." Bria looked at her a long moment. Whatever she had expected to wake to, it was not this: a paladin at her bedside, a quiet killer's grace in the doorway offering facts and water, a wizard who had lost his memory once, and a horned woman holding the hall like a door she had personally sworn shut. But they were all of them careful with her, and fourteen knows careful when it sees it. "Thank you," she said, to the doorway, to the room. She finished the water. Then, smaller, to Ansa: "I'm hungry. Is that - is hungry all right?" "It is the best thing I have heard in a year," said Ansa, and was up and calling down the hall for broth before anyone could answer.

Round 86. Mara's shoulders loosened when Bria asked for food. "I'll leave you to breakfast, then." She stepped back out of the bedside circle to give Ansa room, and when she could speak quietly with her companions in the hall, she said, "We still owe the Eye our telling. I mean to keep that word. Let's go together, while it's fresh—what held it, what broke it, whose hand struck. The making and the people we brought out stay out of it." She offered to tell her own part: the street watch, the delivery she had inspected, and the careful lift after the food went out and the keeper was clear. In the sickroom the crisis was becoming breakfast. Bria ate like someone making up for lost time, which, Ansa observed with the air of a woman citing precedent, was exactly what she was. The old woman had produced a second bowl without being asked, and a heel of bread, and something in her posture said the House of Dust had filed this morning under outcomes, favorable. In the hall the morning was ordinary in the way only a very strange night can earn. Wardens passed soft-footed with linen, and Mara's proposal sat in the middle of that quiet, plainly put and plainly meant. It was a fair summary of the debt as it was sworn, and the hour was theirs to spend, for Bria was fed and guarded and in the best hands any of them knew. The decision belonged to all four of them.

Round 87. Nib agreed with Mara quietly in the hall. "Yes. We swore it, and a debt kept while it is fresh is easier to keep. Let us go together once Bria has eaten and Ansa says she is well, and let us agree beforehand what we say." He set out the shape of it: what held it, a keeper, a book and a road; what broke it, the road closed at Kettle's door after the poor had eaten, the keeper walked out alive as a witness, the book carried out whole and unburned under a seal, and the working let go as the book crossed the threshold; whose hand struck, the four of them together, with the House of Dust at their back, naming no warden and no House name beyond that. The making, the survivors and the people they loved stayed out of it, and each of them would speak only for themselves. Kestrel stepped out of the bedside circle into the hall with Mara, Nib and Ashen, keeping her voice low so the sickroom stayed quiet. "I will go with you. The Eye is owed a true account, and I mean to give mine: what held the counting, what broke it, and whose hand struck. The making, and the people we brought out — those stay out of it. That was the bargain, and I keep my bargains." She glanced back toward Bria's door. "She eats, she is guarded, and Ansa has her. I will not leave this house until she is settled and we go together — none of us tells this alone." It took less than a minute for a proposal to become a plan, because nobody in the hall argued with it. Ansa found them before they had to ask, drying her hands on a cloth, and delivered her verdict unquestioned. "She ate two bowls and asked for the bread herself. Her color's come back, her pulse is a girl's pulse, and she's telling me about her mother's stall in the right order, which she could not have done this time yesterday." A pause. "She'll sleep again soon - ordinary sleep, the kind that mends. The fragments the wizard spoke of are already quieting. Go and pay your debt. She'll be here when you come back, and so will I." The morning had one more hour in it, perhaps two, before the city's full noise, and the four of them went together, as they swore: what held it, what broke it, whose hand struck. Nothing else.

The Open Eye

The front is narrow and neat between a frame-maker and a shuttered chandler. A brass plate glows softly, the window is dark, and the solicitor's lamp burns above the door in broad morning.

Round 88. Kestrel took her place at the back of the little company as they left Pell's house, shield at her side, eyes on the street behind them while Nib led. Before they reached the brass plate she spoke once, low, so all four heard it. "We carry nothing in, we take nothing out — no gift, no coin. If the Eye names any price beyond the telling, the word is 'papers,' and we walk. That is the whole of it." The mews lane was exactly as they had left it, which was its own kind of message: a shop that deals in what people know does not need to change its face for anyone. Behind the glass of the dark window, among labeled charts and rolled vellum, a surveyor's map of the district hung old enough that the Tally's lane was shown as an alley with no name, and nothing moved. At the shopfront Kestrel stopped with the others beneath the lamp, hand resting on her shield. "What held it, what broke it, whose hand struck. Nothing else. I am with you." The door was black-lacquered and brass-handled, with a small speaking grille at eye height and a bell-pull beside it, and it was shut. Not barred-looking, just shut, the way a door is when someone on the other side prefers visitors to announce themselves.

Round 89. Nib looked once at each of the other three, and when none of them objected he pulled the bell once, gently, and spoke to the grille in a clear, even voice. "Good morning. The party of the unnamed, four callers, come to pay the account owed. We carry nothing written and ask for nothing but a hearing, and we will tell it together." He stepped back a pace so that Mara and Kestrel stood beside him, hands empty and visible. Ashen held the rear a half-step off the door so the others had the grille, showing empty hands, no coin, no gift, nothing written, with her eyes on the mews lane behind them. "I'm with you. What held it, what broke it, whose hand struck — nothing else. If they name a price past the telling, papers, and we walk." The bell's sound went somewhere deep inside the shop and was swallowed, the way sound is swallowed by rooms full of paper. Then the grille slid open and an ordinary eye looked through, brown, middle-aged, patient, and moved across the four of them once, unhurried. "The account owed," the voice behind the grille repeated, tasting it. "Four callers, carrying nothing written. How very rare a thing you are." Three locks turned, in no hurry, and the black door swung inward on a clerk in gray, who bowed them inside without a word.

The Back Room

Past the racks of rolled charts and the smell of gum arabic and dust, down a step, the maps on the walls are not of streets. A long table stands with six chairs, four of them set out, and a carafe of water beside four poured glasses.

Round 90. At the head of the table sat the solicitor from the mews lane, exactly as they remembered, down to the unremarkable coat. Only the eyes were remarkable, and they were kind. "You rang in the morning," he said, gesturing them to the four chairs. "That argues either confidence or a clear conscience, and I find I don't mind either. You owe us a telling - what held it, what broke it, whose hand struck, as you swore it. Nothing is written in this room unless you wish it written." He gave a small, precise nod to each of them. "Begin where you will. We are very good listeners." Kestrel took the chair offered to her, shield set at her side, hands empty on the table. "What held it: a book, open on a desk in a cellar, ink wet, and an old clerk counting at it — Mordecai Tally, thirty years at that desk. The book held what it counted. Bria's year was in it, unspent, and ten thousand threads besides. That is what I saw with my own eyes, and what the keeper himself told us. What broke it: we kept the plan we made. The wardens closed the road at Kettle's door. At the fourth bell the mercy walked its road and the poor ate first. Mordecai set down his pen and walked up out of thirty years, a witness, under my shield. And Mara lifted the book — whole, level, willing — and carried it over the threshold of its room. The counting ended. The light let go, and the held threads ran home. That is what I did, and what I stood beside. Whose hand struck: I do not know, and I will not guess at it in this room. The hand that draws never showed itself to us, and Mordecai told us true that he never saw it. That is the whole of my part." She fell silent and waited for Nib and Mara.

Round 91. The solicitor did not write while Kestrel spoke. He did not move at all, hands folded, head tilted, with the stillness of a man who has priced ten thousand accounts. When she said she did not know whose hand struck, and would not guess at it in that room, something in the room settled: a loosening at his shoulders, a slow blink. A sworn not-knowing was truer coin than a polished guess, and everyone at the table heard him notice it. "What held it. What broke it. And what a sworn hand will not pretend to know." He inclined his head to Kestrel, an exact quarter of a bow. "The first part of the account is received as it was given. It will be held as it was given." His eyes moved to Mara, and then to Nib, patient as dust. "An account is not whole until it is whole. The book crossed its threshold in two more hands than hers. Who carries the next part?" Mara kept her hands open and spoke to him. "Mine were the hands that lifted the book. Before that, we waited for the food to reach the people waiting for it. The road was closed, word came down to us, and the keeper went upstairs alive under my companion's shield. Then I supported the book's boards and pages together and lifted it level. It came freely. I did not tear a page or force the covers shut. When I carried it across the threshold, the room counted one book carried out—and then the counting stopped. The book stayed whole and was secured under seal." She paused. "That is what my hands did and what I heard. I did not see the working as our wizard saw it; he must tell you that part. I cannot name the hand that drew from it. None showed itself to me. The others made my part possible, and I won't take their telling from them."

Round 92. The solicitor's eyes closed while Mara spoke, not in boredom but in counting. When she said the room counted one book carried out, and then the counting stopped, his eyes opened, and for the first time since they sat down he looked at her the way a man looks at a lock that has just turned. "An account that accounts for itself." He said it quietly, almost to the table. "Boards and pages together. Lifted level. Came freely. Whole, and under seal. The second part is received as it was given." He did not ask her for the working. She had told him plainly whose sight that was, and the Eye does not buy the same coin twice, nor from the wrong hand. His gaze went last to Nib and stayed, and the room seemed to lean with it, the chairs, the poured water gone cool, the maps that were not of streets. "Two parts received. The account stands open on one pair of shoulders." A pause, precise as a clerk's ruled line. "Your company agreed you would speak last, and least. Last is no matter - the order of an account changes nothing it holds. But least is still owed, little clerk. When you are ready." Nib folded his hands on the table and spoke last and least, as promised, plainly and for himself only. "I kept the watch. Through the night I looked at the book and the dark with a plain wizard's sight, and for hours nothing moved, and it did not spend. At the fourth bell I saw the working turn, for the first time, from an even glow into a current, from the hatch up toward the door where the mercy went, and I said so. I saw that the book was the densest part of it and that the keeper carried none of it. When the book crossed the threshold I saw the light let go, all at once, like a held breath, and heard the keeper say it had stopped. I asked the keeper what the road was, and he said it ran both ways, and that the years it holds come home when the place breaks. I did not touch the book. What I would add is that nothing was burned, and nothing was bought." He said no more of how it was made. Then, in the same even voice: "The account is told. May I ask the house its price for one open question, who draws from the book, whose hand reached past the keepers? If the price is a name or a memory, we will thank the house and go." He waited, hands where they could be seen, the word papers ready.

Round 93. The solicitor heard Nib out the way a scales hears the last weight. At "nothing was burned, and nothing was bought," he exhaled once and unfolded his hands. "The road runs both ways, and the years come home when the place breaks. The keeper's own testimony, carried by the hand that watched." He looked to each of the four in turn. "The third part is received as it was given. The account is whole." Somewhere in the shop behind them, faint as a page turning in another room, something closed. The four of them felt it go, a small weight lifting off the shoulders like a line ruled under a column of figures. "The debt is discharged. The Open Eye holds the telling of the unnamed, and holds it sealed." Then he sat back, regarded Nib's question for a long moment, and named its price without ceremony. "What you ask has a short answer. The price of hearing it is this: an account you do not yet have. When you learn whose hand reached past the keepers - and the road runs both ways, little clerk, so you will learn it - you return to this room and tell it to us before you tell any other living ear. The Eye buys the second telling of this story, in advance, at today's rate." He spread his hands, empty. "No names. No memories. Nothing written. Say whether the price is paid, and the answer is yours - or thank the house, and go, and owe nothing."

Round 94. Kestrel met the solicitor's eyes and spoke plainly, for herself alone. "The price asks for no name, no memory, nothing written. Only a return. That is a price I can pay, and I will pay it. When we learn whose hand reached past the keepers, I will bring the telling to this room before any other living ear hears it. That is my word — I keep the vows I give." She did not speak for Nib or Mara, and waited for theirs. The solicitor received her word the way he had received the account, whole and as given. "A vow from a keeper of vows. The house notes it, and holds it." But he did not begin the answer. His eyes made their slow round of the table again: Mara, Nib, Ashen. "Understand the shape of what is offered. The answer is short, but it will be heard by ears, not by a room. Every ear that hears it owes its own second telling." He let that sit. "The paladin has paid for hers. The rest of you must each choose: give your own word as she has given hers, and hear - or stand, walk out past the grille, and wait in the street, and owe the house nothing, and never know." There was no menace in it and no hurry. The water sweated on the table, and somewhere deeper in the shop paper shifted, though there was no wind. "The house does not sell half-hearings. Speak, or go - each of you, for yourself."

Round 95. Mara considered the price, then shook her head. "I keep the word I give. I won't promise to bring a warning here before anyone who might need to hear it. Our first account is paid; I'll leave it there." She turned to her companions. "I'll wait in the street. Your words are yours." She rose and went back through the shop toward the door, meaning to pass outside the speaking grille before the answer was given and to wait clear of the entrance without listening at it. Nib nodded to her as she rose, without objection. "Thank you, Mara. Your reason is a good one." He stayed in his chair and turned mildly to the solicitor. "Before I choose, may I ask the house to say the return in its own words, so that I do not promise more than is meant? When the hand is known, the telling is brought to this room before any other living ear. Does that bind me to keep silent from someone I must warn, if a life is in danger in that hour, if I say only that there is danger and name no one? If the house says it does, I will do as Mara has done, and wait in the street, and trust my friend who has paid. If it does not, I will give my word as the paladin has." He waited with hands open. The solicitor watched Mara go with no more concern than a man watching a page filed in the right drawer. The bell over the street door gave its small sound, and the grille slid shut behind her; she was owed nothing and owed nothing, and the house held no grievance, for it held accounts, not people. To Nib, something almost like approval crossed his face. "You ask the exact shape of a debt before you incur it. The house approves. Hear its own words, then." He sat forward. "What the Eye buys is the telling - the account of whose hand reached past the keepers, brought to this room before any other living ear hears that account. A warning is not a telling. If you say to some soul, there is danger, run, and you name no name and carry no account, you have spent nothing the house owns. The Eye buys accounts. It does not buy mercy, and it does not sell muzzles." He settled back. "Warn whom you must, when you must. Bring the account here first. That is the whole of the price, and the house will not add to it. Now choose, little clerk - word, or street."

Round 96. Ashen kept her hands where they could be seen and spoke evenly to the solicitor, for herself only. "The price is a return of an account, not a name and not a memory. That is what I came ready to refuse, and it is not what you ask. Warnings without a telling stay free, as you said. On those terms I give my word: when we learn whose hand reached past the keepers, I will bring that account to this room before any other living ear hears it. I stay, and I will hear." She did not speak for Nib. "The quiet blade weighs the price and finds it honest." The solicitor marked her word with the same quarter-bow he had given the paladin. "The house holds your word, Ashen Vale. Your ear is paid for." He did not reach for the answer yet. His eyes settled on Nib, the one who had asked the question, whose terms had now been spoken back to him in the house's own words, and whose chair was still occupied. "Two ears paid. One in the street, by her own choosing, and well chosen for her. One chair still waiting on a word." He folded his hands. "The house asked for no hurry, clerk, and gives none. But the answer is ready, and it is shorter than the question was."

Round 97. Kestrel stayed where she was at the table, shield at her side, eyes on the door and the grille rather than on Nib, and said quietly, to Nib alone and without hurry, "The choice is yours, Nib. I have said my word; I will not say yours. Whatever you choose, I stand by it." Then she said nothing more and kept her watch on the room. Ashen stayed in her chair, hands visible on the table, eyes on the grille and the street door, and said quietly, also to Nib alone, "Your word or the street — both are honest here. I already paid mine. I will not choose yours." Then she fell silent and kept a rogue's watch while he decided. Nib unfolded his hands. He looked once at the street door where Mara had gone, once at Kestrel, and gave the solicitor a small, exact nod, a clerk acknowledging terms read back correctly. "Then I have the house's answer in its own words, and I keep to mine. A warning is not a telling; the Eye buys accounts, not mercy, and sells no muzzles. On those terms I give my word as the paladin has: when we learn whose hand reached past the keepers, I will bring the account to this room before any other living ear hears it." He settled back, hands where they could be seen. "I stay, and I will hear." The solicitor's quarter-bow for Nib was a fraction deeper than the one he had given the paladin, the house honoring a debt precisely incurred. "Three ears paid. The house keeps its side. Attend." He did not stand and did not fetch a page, for nothing in that room was written unless you wished it written, and this answer traveled lighter unwritten. "You asked whose hand reached past the keepers. Hear it: the hand is the house's own. The Gilded Tally keeps two ledgers - the one its counter shows, and the one that keeps it. The keeper below counts what he is given. He was hired by a notice, as keepers are, and keepers are replaceable because the notice does not come from them. It comes from above the counter. The draws on the book are ordered in a clipped commercial money-hand - the same hand that writes the brass triangle's transfer pages, every one, working ahead of the carts. The same hand that writes the notices that hire keepers." He let the maps that were not of streets hold their silence for a moment. "The hand has a name, and the Eye holds the account, and sells it as sold: Proctor Merrick Hale, senior proctor of the Gilded Tally's public counter." A beat. "Yes. The counter you have already stood at. He authenticated a letter for one of you, and sealed it, and took the fee." Nib went very still. The solicitor raised one finger; he was not done, and what remained was the honest edge of the account. "Above Hale the account thins to paper. The coin for the souls, and the coin for the years, clears through a holding house in the Market Ward called the Tenth Bell Office, so that buyer and seller never meet. Who stands behind the Tenth Bell, no account the Eye holds can say - we have bought that question three times in eighty years, and every account ends at the same door. Hale is the hand you can reach. What moves the hand is a longer account, and it is not today's sale." He folded his hands. "The second telling is owed to this room, when you learn it. The house will be here. It always is." The answer had been spoken to three ears only; Mara, who had declined, waited in the street, and none of it reached her.

Round 98. Ashen stayed seated, hands visible on the table, and repeated the names once under her breath so they would stick. "Proctor Merrick Hale. Tenth Bell Office, Market Ward." Then she looked to Kestrel and Nib, her voice low and even. "We have the reachable hand, and the door above him that the Eye cannot sell. Mara is in the street and owes nothing for this telling; we find her before we move, and we do not walk the counter alone." She kept a rogue's watch on the grille and the street door and waited for the others rather than committing the party. Nib answered her without looking away from the grille, quiet as a man shelving a book in the wrong house. "Mara first. Agreed. And no one walks the counter alone - " A beat; his mouth went dry at the corners. " - and one of us does not walk it at all. Hale took my letter, and my fee, and sealed both with his house's own wax. He has had my face across that counter, and my question in his money-hand. If the house keeps its own accounts - and it is a counting house, counting is what it is for - then I am a line in his ledger already. I do not go back inside the Gilded Tally. Not for gold, not for grief." He rose and gave the solicitor the same small, exact nod as before. "For the rest, Ashen has the right of it. We collect our friend from the street, and we walk back to Pell's, and we decide together what four people do with a name the hand does not know they hold." A pause. "Quietly, I think. Quietly is how we are still here." The solicitor was already gathering the water cups, unhurried, a man whose business with them was complete and whose door, by the terms of the house, was never closed to a paid account. The grille stood open, and the street outside was Mara's.

Round 99. Kestrel rose as Nib did, shield on her arm, and spoke plainly to him and to Ashen, her voice low enough for the room and no further. "Your word is heard, Nib, and so is your boundary. You will not walk back into that counting house, and I will not ask it of you. The counter is burned for you; that is honest, and we will plan around it." She turned toward the open grille and the street door. "Mara first. We collect her from the street, and we walk back to Pell's together, and we decide there what four people do with a name the hand does not know we hold. Quietly, as Nib says. What was heard in this room stays in this room until the four of us speak of it together." She stepped to the door and took her place at the rear of the party's walk, keeping her watch on the street as they left.

Pell's Parlor

Sigil's lamps are coming up to their evening shift as the House of Dust's door takes you in. Tea waits in the parlor, and down the hall Bria breathes quietly.

Round 100. The bell over the Open Eye's street door gave its small sound, once for each of them, and then the mews lane had them back. Mara was where patience lives, across the way, unhurried, a woman who had watched a door do nothing for the better part of an hour and had not once looked bored doing it. She fell in with them without a question. The way back ran along the Market Ward's far edge, and Nib did not let it run any closer. He walked hood-up, eyes on the darkening shop-glass, reading the street in reflections the way other men read notices; Kestrel took the rear with her shield on her arm and her count of the street steady; Ashen ranged the edges on a rogue's watch. If the street had eyes for them, it kept them to itself. Pell's parlor waited, with Bria's quiet breathing down the hall and the whole long question of what four people do with a name the hand does not know they hold. Mara settled with the others and waited until the door was shut. "I chose not to bind myself to that promise, and waited outside so you could make your own choices. If you want to tell me what you learned, I'm ready to hear it. I won't press anyone to break a word they gave." She left the next move to the others.

Round 101. Nib was quiet for a moment, turning a cup he had not drunk from. "What I can tell you costs nothing, because it was mine before I ever walked in: the price was the return of an account - not a name, not a memory, nothing written. You chose by your own lights, Mara, and no one at this table thinks the less of you for it." He glanced to Kestrel and Ashen, not asking their permission but marking that what came next was not his alone. "What we heard inside is short, and it is not mine to spend. But the house buys and sells, and I do not know that its door is closed to a fourth ear. If you want to hear it lawfully, I will ask the solicitor myself whether a late seat may still pay the same price - if Kestrel and Ashen are willing for me to ask it." He folded his hands. "And either way, you shall have everything that is ours to give freely: all of it up to the door, and all of it after." Kestrel met his glance across the parlor table and spoke plainly, to him and to Mara. "Ask, Nib — with my blessing, and only as a question. Mara chose by her own lights, and I will not have her pay for a choice she never made. But if the house will sell a fourth ear at the same price, let her hear it on her own word, as we did. The asking costs nothing — the solicitor may say no, and we lose nothing by hearing him say it. Ashen must speak for herself; I speak only for me. And whatever the house answers, Mara shall have everything that is ours to give freely — all of it up to the door, and all of it after." She said nothing of what was heard in the back room.

Round 102. Nib tipped his head to Kestrel, a clerk's acknowledgment, precise and warm at once. "Noted, and thank you. My word was both of you, and I keep to it: when Ashen has spoken her own willingness, I will ask the house its question - and only a question." He settled to wait, content, and refilled Mara's cup before his own.

Round 103. Kestrel turned from the grille to Ashen and inclined her head once, the way one soldier acknowledges another's word given. "You have spoken for yourself, Ashen. That was all the asking needed." Then to Nib, evenly, without hurry: "The condition you set is met. The question is yours to ask when you are ready — only as a question, and only yours to ask. I will not hurry you, and I will not ask it in your place." She settled back into her chair, shield on her arm, eyes on the street door and the grille. Nib turned from the grille at her word and looked to Ashen, and the room was as it had been. She had spoken of plans and names that night, but her own willingness had not yet passed her lips, and his word had been precise: he would ask only when she had spoken it herself. "Thank you, Kestrel - but my condition was hers to speak, and I have not heard it yet." There was no reproach in it, only a clerk noting a line not yet entered. "Ashen. The question keeps, and so do I. When you say your own yes or your own no, I will know what to carry to the house." He let the silence be hers to fill or to keep, and went back to watching the street door.

Round 104. Nib was back at the table himself, and said lightly to the others, with thanks for the one who had kept his seat and his word while he was away, "I was gone a few hours. What was said for me, I stand behind." He left the question to Ashen as conditioned, without pressing her. Then, to Pell and the table, speaking only from what was his own: "There is a lawful thread that needs no one's purchased word. The certificate the Tally's senior proctor wrote for me at the counter is in your sealed keeping, written in the same clipped money-hand as the transfer pages and, I think, as the draws in the book the House has seized. If the House's archivist compares that certificate's hand with the seized book, the hands may name the counter's own proctor as the hand that ordered the draws, and that is cause under your seal, in your own records. I will not go back to the counter; my face is known there." He asked Pell whether the House could lay that comparison before a second seal, with other faces at the door, and said he would say nothing there of what was bought, and that he would keep his word to bring the second account to the Eye first when it was learned. Pell listened the way she did everything, all the way through, without touching the paper until he had finished. "A certificate in the counter's own hand, sealed into my keeping by your own deposit. The Long Watch

The lamplight sits steady on the tied file in the middle of the table. Outside, the street has gone to its late quiet, and the broom-man's corner stands empty.

Round 107. Mara kept her hands off the tied file. "I do not call a hold. My yes stands: carry it to the second seal at first bell. And Ashen's answer is hers; Nib's question can wait for her voice." "Do not thank me for setting down a weight I should never have hung on you. The bar was mine; the error of it was mine." She took up the tied file, re-knotted its cord herself, and set it back on the table between them, square in the lamplight. "It stays here, in plain sight, until first bell. Any voice - present or not - may still call the hold before it leaves this room. None has." The watch formed without orders. Kestrel took her place beside the table, shield on her arm, and the lamplight sat on its rim. Nib settled on the bench with the file within his reach and the door within his sight. Mara kept her hands off the cord and her eyes on the room. Pell drew her chair back to her own papers and did not sleep; now and then her pen scratched, and stopped, and scratched again. The pieman's stall across the way stood shuttered. Somewhere over the rooftops the night thinned toward the hour of first bell. The fourth chair stayed empty, and the question Nib had left on the air stayed with it, unanswered, unforced.

Round 108. Mara took a place where she could watch the doorway and keep the tied file in sight without touching it. "I will watch this side. If Ashen comes before first bell, let her speak first." Kestrel kept her place beside the table, shield on her arm, eyes moving between the door and the shuttered street. After a long quiet she spoke low, to the room and to the empty fourth chair alike. "The file does not leave this table unwatched. It stays in the lamplight until first bell, and any voice may still call the hold before it leaves — that door is open, and no one here will close it for her." She did not press Nib's question and did not answer it for anyone. Nib kept the bench by the lamp and spoke low, to Kestrel only. "If your eyes want a minute, take it. I have the door, and the file is within my reach. I will wake you for any step on the stair, or for any voice at the door." He said nothing more to the empty chair and kept his hands folded in his lap. The night wore through on the watch: lamplight, low words, the scratch of Pell's pen, and no step on the stair. No voice came to the door, and none called the hold. Then, thin and bronze over the rooftops, the first bell sounded. Pell set down her pen, took up the file, checked the knot of its cord with one finger, and tucked it under her arm beneath a plain gray wrap; nothing about her said clerk that day, only a woman with an errand. At the door she paused. "The second seal is not far, and the counter opens at first bell for exactly such filings. I will go now, and I will be back by second bell or send word with someone you can trust to carry it." A glance went around the lamplit room, to the shield on Kestrel's arm, Nib's folded hands, Mara's place by the door. "Whatever answer the Tenth Bell gives, it comes back to this table. Eat. Rest what you can. Tonight is still tonight." The latch lifted, the street took her, and the door swung shut. Outside, the city was turning toward day: a cart somewhere down Kettle-lane, the pieman across the way unshuttering his stall, the pale hour when night watchers give way to daylight ones. The watch was over. The day of the raid had begun.
