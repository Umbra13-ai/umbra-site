The Counting Room

Twenty steps down, lamplight pools over a single desk where an old man with inked sleeves sits before an open book, and water-marked pages lie in stacked trays. A voice with no mouth counts every soul that crosses the floor, too steady to be a person's. Past the reach of the lamps the dark begins, and it does not say what it holds.

Round 65. Nib came down the twenty steps with one hand on the wall, and the counting took him in as it had taken the others: "- and one more, come down from the street. Eleven thousand four hundred and six - and three." The last minute of his Detect Magic was still on him at the foot of the stair, and it was like opening his eyes underwater. The room SHONE: not the lamps but everything, stone, air, stacked trays and water-marked pages, all wearing one dim luminance, a working not laid on the room but worn by it, the way old cloth is worn by the body it outlived. The book on the desk was the same light, only denser, not a source but a knot where whatever this was gathered tightest. The old man was only a man, inked sleeves and no more shine on him than the stool he sat on, and the dark past the lamps read exactly like the light, for the working did not thin with distance. Then, between one breath and the next, the spell was gone and the room was a room again. Nib tried to make sense of what it had shown him and could not; the chord the room was sounding would not separate into notes for him, and he was honest enough with himself not to invent a structure he did not have.

So he asked his two questions mildly, and Mordecai Tally answered them the way he had set his pen down: exactly. "The road." A slow nod, as if it were the first good coin of the night. "It is not a street. The carts use the street; the road uses the carts. Mercy walks it one way, from Kettle's door to this room. What is drawn walks it the other, from them to the book. Two directions, one path - and no, it is not laid in the stones, or some clever man with a pick would have broken it a hundred years back." He spread his inked hands. "That is all I know of it, which is more than any keeper before me thought to ask." Then: "The book holds what it counts, the way a well holds water - you heard me say it. What is drawn off it goes where the counting sends it, and where that is, I never asked. A keeper who asks where the count goes stops being a keeper and starts being a debtor." Something older and tireder looked out of him for a moment. "I have watched what becomes of the debtors. They are most of my pages." Nib, at Mara's shoulder, murmured that she did not have to answer "keeper's daughter," and the old man gave neither press nor retreat; he only waited, palms open, while the tally stood at eleven thousand four hundred and six, and three.

The West Yard

The lane to the delivery yard runs empty both ways. The yard gate stands shut over cart ruts frozen into the ground, and across from the Tally's door the broom-man's post is empty. Kettle's door is dark.

Round 66. On the street above, Ashen traded the cistern step for a slow drift along the west-yard line, and the night gave her the honest little it had. The lane to the delivery yard ran empty both ways. The yard gate stood shut with fresh cart ruts frozen under it and no cart at it, and across from the Tally's door the broom-man's post stayed empty at that hour. Kettle's door was dark, and nobody walked the mercy road toward her or away. The watch held, and now it held the west approach too.

Round 67. Kestrel planted her feet at the front of the four, shield raised toward the dark past the lamps, and opened the paladin's sense. It reached sixty feet, through lamplight and into the dark, and it answered the only way it could: nothing. (Divine Sense: no celestial, no fiend, no undead within sixty feet.) The old man was a man, inked and tired and plain to it, and what else the dark kept the sense did not say, because it never said more than its three kinds; anything behind true cover, or of any other make, would read the same as empty. The silence was a bound, not an answer. Mordecai Tally watched her do it the way a man watches weather he has seen before, and she put her two questions to him in the voice of the sworn sword, speaking for herself alone: "I heard you, keeper. I will not break the book - it holds other people's years, and I came to save them, not bury them. I will not break the hand - you said yourself another would come. So I ask about what can be stopped without burying them. Two things. First: you say the book spends what it holds only when someone draws. Who draws? Whose hand reaches in and spends what it holds - yours, or a hand above yours? Second: the years in that book belong to living people. Bria sleeps under wardens with the taking still working in her, and there are others. Are they spent, or held?"

He answered them exactly, in order. "Who draws." He said it flat, tasting it. "Not me. Never me. I have never drawn, and I have never seen the hand that does. When the book spends, I count the spending after - I am never in the room when a drawing is done. Whoever reaches in, they reach past keepers. It has always been so; it is why keepers die in their beds." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Ask what is above me. I cannot answer. I was not hired to know, and I learned young not to become the kind of man who is told." Then he looked at the open book, and his voice went careful. "The years. Held, mistress, if no one has drawn on them. What is held comes back when the counting breaks - the old cases say it, and my own pages say it. What is spent does not come back. Nothing comes back from a spending." He turned one page back, gently, and showed her the fresh wet lines without touching them. "Your sleeping girl - her year is here, unspent. No draw has touched it. I would have counted the drawing." The pen stayed down, the tally stood, and the counting went on without hands to do it.

Round 68. Mara's fingers tightened on the strap of her tool pouch, and she put it to him plain. "You called me a keeper's daughter. Which keeper do you mean? What do you know about me?" Mordecai Tally received it the way he had received all their questions that night, palms open and nothing held. "No keeper in particular, mistress. And nothing about you that you have not shown me yourself, tonight." A small, precise shrug. "I name what I see. It is a clerk's habit, and an old one. You said my name the way a woman says it when she learned young how a counting-room sounds before it speaks - how the pause before an answer tells you whether the answer is true. Someone kept books near you, once, and you listened." The inked hands turned up, empty. "If I read it wrong, then it is only an old man's guess, and you may leave it on the desk beside the pen." He did not press. The tally stood at eleven thousand four hundred and six, and three, and the dark past the lamps kept its own counsel.

Round 69. Kestrel pressed the one fork nobody had asked about, holding her post at the front with her shield toward the dark. "You named three things that keep a counting, keeper. The hand, the book - and the road the mercy walks. I have asked you about the first two. Now I ask about the third, because it is the one no one has ever asked about, and I mean to ask it plain. First: what is the road? Where does it run, who walks it, and what comes through that hatch tonight - this mercy that is still unopened? Second: if the counting is broken by stopping the road instead of the book or the hand, what happens to the years the book holds - do they come back to the people they belong to, Bria and the others - and what happens to the mercy itself? I will not break what buries them. Tell me what the road is, so we can choose what to break." As she listened for the answer she read him closely. (Insight 9 + 3 = 12 vs DC 10: success.) For the first time all night Mordecai Tally was quiet before he answered, not the quiet of a man choosing a lie but of a man putting down a weight he had carried a long time without ever setting it where another could see. He was telling what he believed, no more and no less, though what he believed might still be wrong; a sense reads the man, not the truth.

"What comes through the hatch tonight is bread," he said. "Sack bread, salt fish, a physician's parcel wrapped in waxed cloth. Tonight's lies unopened; I open and log every mercy myself, have done for years. And then it goes back up, mistress. That is the shape of it, and I have never had a better answer than the shape. The alms come down through this room - every crate, every sack, the long way through a locked cellar - and Kettle's door above gives them away. The mercy walks from Kettle's door to this room, and then it walks back, and the poor eat. Why the road runs through the counting place at all, no keeper has ever been told. I log what walks. I was not hired to ask why it walks here." He folded his inked hands. "I will not spend your hope on a guess, so I will tell you exactly where my knowledge ends. Nobody has ever broken the road. Nobody has ever asked what it is - until you. The years that came back, in the old cases and in my own pages, came back when a counting PLACE broke. Whether the road is part of the place or a fourth thing standing beside the three I named - I cannot say. If the road stops, I know only this: the mercy stops with it, Kettle's door goes dark, and the street will notice that inside a week. What the drawn shares do when their walking is blocked, no page of mine records." The pen stayed down and the tally stood, while tonight's mercy waited in its crate behind the west-wall hatch, unopened.

Round 70. Mara let the old man's guess about her lie, and turned to the west-wall hatch instead. "Then let's see what actually came down that road." The crate and the bag waited beneath it, still cinched from tonight's carrying, and the keeper only watched her cross to them, mild, palms open, a man who logged what walked and did not mind it walking. She worked the bag's fastening loose without cutting anything, exactly as she meant to. It was a clerk's knot, neat and practiced and meant to be untied and retied without fraying the cord, with a tally-stick tied into it at the neck, one notch for the crate and one for the bag in the same small cut-hand as the book's wet lines. (Investigation 2 + 3 = 5 vs DC 12: failure; she finds no accounting.) Inside was only what he had said: sack bread gone a day stale, salt fish in paper, and a physician's parcel in waxed cloth, corded tight. She turned each over and read the cord, the wax, the tally-stick and the seam, but if the road kept a secret inside its bread and fish, it kept it better than her hands could pick out tonight. There was no label, no mark, no packing that argued with the old man's description. It was a bag of alms, and it would close again with no harm done to any of it. "It goes back up before the fourth bell, mistress," Mordecai said behind her, neither hurried nor hindering. "Tie the knot as you found it, and I shall write that it was opened by a guest and found honest. It will be the first time any mercy was ever checked against my word." Almost a smile. "I find I do not mind being checked."

Round 71. Nib thanked Mara for opening the bag so carefully and finding it honest, and then spoke to the room, steadily. "What I think I have heard, and tell me where I am wrong. The years held in the book come back when a counting place breaks, and the spent ones do not. Break the book, and what it holds is buried. So we must not break it. We must break the place, and a place is a hand, a book and a road. I would not burn anything." To Mordecai, mildly: "One question. If the hand is gone from this room, the road is blocked at Kettle's door so no mercy and no drawing walks it, and the book is carried out whole under the House's seal, does the counting stop, or is any one of the three enough to keep it going? And if you were taken out of this room as a witness under the House's protection, would another hand come to take your place before the seal is set on the door?" He laid out the order he proposed to Mara, Kestrel and Pell: first, Pell's wardens would quietly close the road at Kettle's door with the cart men and watch for the one who draws; second, Mordecai would go up as a witness under the House's protection while Pell sealed the room; last of all, and only after the other two, Mara would lift the book whole into a case under the seal, with Kestrel beside her and Nib watching the room with Detect Magic ready. He asked that the survivors be kept near the House and the book, because he did not know where the years returned, and he named "papers" as the word that would stop everything if the book began to spend.

The old man listened the way he had listened to everything that night, head a little bowed, as if Nib were reading him a page he had never been shown of a book he had kept for years. "You ask whether any one of the three is enough to keep a counting going, and I will tell you what a man can know who has only ever kept one." He raised one inked finger. "The hand is not enough. You have seen it yourselves - my pen has been down this half of the night, and the room has counted you crossing, every one, without a hand to do it. And I am the second proof: the keeper before me died in his bed, and the counting did not stop for his dying. It waited, and another hand came, and the hand was me." A pause. "I answered a notice, as I remember it. It was a long time ago, and I do not remember answering it very clearly." Of the book and the road, he said, no living man had the evidence; he had never seen the room without its book, which could not be replaced, and no one had ever blocked the road. "What you describe, master, is not one break but three at once, and no counting place in my pages ever faced such a night. If anything under this sky ends a counting, it may be exactly that. I cannot promise it." The inked hands opened, empty. "And if you take me up those stairs a witness, then yes - if a hand is wanted here, another will come. One always has. Who sends them, ask what is above me. I learned young not to become the kind of man who is told." He looked once to Mara, once to Kestrel, and last to the open book with its wet lines. "Whatever you break, break it after the fourth bell has counted the mercy back up into the street. The poor have no part in my pages. Let them eat first."

Round 72. Above, Ashen stayed on outside watch and did not descend. Knowing Nib wanted the road closed at Kettle's door first, she widened her circuit along the west-yard line toward the street end of the mercy road, moving the way she had moved all night, slow and unhurried, a shape the street had already stopped noticing. She was looking to see whether the door was still dark and whether anyone, cart-men, a watcher or a stranger who might be the drawing hand, stood near it. The street gave her the obvious, and only the obvious. (Perception 9 + 0 = 9 vs DC 12: failure.) Kettle's door was still dark, its grille unlit, and the lane ran empty both ways with the yard gate shut over ruts that had seen no cart that night and the broom-man's post still empty. If the drawing hand meant to walk tonight, it had not walked past her yet, or it walked in a way her eyes were not finding, and some part of her, honest as a good scout should be, could not swear the difference. The lane would take Pell's wardens quietly, that much she could say, for empty was empty and the cover was what it had been. She settled back into the circuit with her hands off steel and kept the watch.

Round 73. Kestrel gave her word, plain and whole and with the shield already up. "Keeper. I have heard you - all of it. Your pen is down, and still the room counts; the hand is not enough, and you have never seen the one who draws. I will not ask you again what you do not know. Here is my word instead. The mercy goes out first. I will hold this stair until the fourth bell has rung and the poor have eaten, and no break of ours will take bread from them - unless the hand that draws moves first, and then I move with it. And you will walk up out of this room alive, keeper, under my shield, and you will speak what you know to the wardens as a witness. No one here will lay a hand on you while I stand. That is my vow, and I keep my vows." Mordecai Tally received it as a man receives something he has not dared to ask for twice. He had asked once, for the poor, and she had answered with more than he asked. He bowed his head over his inked hands, and when he lifted it there was something settled in his face that had not been there all night. "Then I am in your keeping, mistress," he said. "And I find I am not afraid of it. I have written ten thousand debts in this book and never once seen the other side of a vow - the side that costs the maker something."

He set both palms flat on the desk. "Here is mine, since vows are being given: when your wardens come, I will go up those stairs quietly, and I will speak to your House as a witness, and I will hide nothing that my pages know. My testimony is the only coin I have ever held that was mine to spend. I spend it." He told her the shape of the hours as he understood them, that she must keep the room through the whole span until the fourth bell or leave it and return, and he could not say which was safer, having never seen either tried. And one last thing, gently, as a man says a thing he does not enjoy knowing: "I cannot promise the room will be as patient as you are. If the hand comes to draw before your bell - if it comes tonight - the counting will do what it does, and I have never once seen it stopped. What that looks like from where you stand, I cannot say. No one has ever stood where you are standing."

DM Ruling. The old man stopped himself, one inked hand lifted, and laughed once, dry and at his own expense, correcting his count of the hours. "No. Forgive me. Hear how a keeper's habits speak for him - I said the day's work, because the day is what other folk keep. Below, the night's work is ours. You did not come down at first light; you came down at full dark, and not an hour since, with the mercy cart still creaking at the yard gate above. And the fourth bell is no day away. It rings tonight, before dawn, as it always rings - and Kettle's door gives at it, and the poor eat at it, tonight. The mercy goes up before the bell, mistress, and the bell is hours off, no more. Hold your vow and you hold it against hours, not against a day. I would not have you swear to more than I asked."

Round 74. Mara put tonight's mercy back the way it had come, bread and fish and waxed parcel, and worked the clerk's knot closed again with its own cord, the tally-stick riding in it where it had ridden before. "Let tonight's food go up. I agree to Nib's order after that: close the road, bring the keeper out as a witness, and only then try moving the book whole. I know none of us can promise what that will do." She turned to Kestrel. "I'll stay here with you until the bell. If someone comes to draw first, we face that together." It was honest work, though not a clerk's knot; Mordecai Tally watched her fingers with a professional's sympathy and anyone could have seen him find the difference. "Near enough for anyone but me," he said, "and I do not count, because I shall write it plain: opened by a guest, found honest, retied by the same hand." The pen lifted for the first time in half the night, wrote one short line, and set itself down again. "There. The bag is honest twice over now."

She called her assent up the stair, and Brother Tam took it up without a question: her agreement, the timing of the bell, and the order of it, road first, keeper second, book last, and nothing closed before the poor had eaten. The answer came back down within the minute in Tam's even voice, from the front room where Pell kept the seal. "The solicitor hears. The seal waits on your word, and the word waits on the bell. He says a patient house is harder to fight than a hungry one." Then there was nothing to do but what they had chosen. The pen stayed down and the tally stood, and the keeper folded his hands and waited with them while the night wore on toward the fourth bell.

Round 75. Nib sat on the bottom step with his back to the wall and his spellbook closed on his knee, and kept the long watch while the others waited. He cast Detect Magic as a ritual, no slot, again and again, ten minutes at a time, each time looking only at the book and the dark past the lamps for any change in the light. (Detect Magic, ritual: no slot spent.) The room wore the working as old cloth wears the body: stone, air and trays all dimly and evenly luminous, and the book on the desk the same light gathered to its knot, no tighter than before and no looser. No thread left anyone, and nothing in reach of the spell moved, tightened or began. The dark past the lamps lay beyond the spell's short reach as surely as beyond the lamps', and the light touched what it touched, no more. Nib told Kestrel and Mara he would say "papers" at once if the book began to spend, and asked them to take turns resting, promising to wake whoever slept the moment the light shifted. Above him the two women traded the watch between them with their eyes, one upright while the other rested, and the keeper sat at his desk with his pen down and his hands folded, patient as furniture. An hour went by that way, whisper and light, whisper and light, and gave back exactly nothing, which was what they had asked of it.

Round 76. Kestrel took the upright watch at the foot of the stair by the wall hatch, where one slow turn of her head covered the book, the hatch and the way up, shield on her arm and longsword sheathed. She spoke quietly, to Mara first: "Rest your eyes. I'll hold the stair - the spell's light comes and goes, and my eyes don't need it." Then to the keeper, low and even: "Pen down, keeper. We count nothing tonight but the hours." The second hour of the long watch had something the first had not, eyes that did not go dark when the spell did. Nib's whisper rose and fell on the bottom step, the light came and went with it, and through every dark gap Kestrel's gaze did the work that needed no magic. The hatch stayed shut with tonight's mercy retied beneath it, and the stair stayed empty. The book lay open under its knot of light and did not spend. Mara rested her eyes against the wall with her rapier across her knees, breathing like a woman who has learned to sleep with one ear open, and the keeper watched them watch; once, meeting Kestrel's eyes on her circuit, he mouthed three words without a sound: I would count. Three alarms were better than one, the lamps, the spell and the keeper's counting, and the second hour passed with nothing moving, nothing tightening, nothing beginning.

Round 77. Mara rose from the wall, settled her rapier back in its sheath, and said quietly to Kestrel, "My turn. Rest your eyes if you want." She took an upright place in the lamplight from which she could watch the book, the west hatch and the stair. "Watch on," she told the room. The third hour of the watch was the quietest yet, because quiet is a thing that compounds. Nib's whisper and light went on, whisper and light. Kestrel stood by the stair with her eyes shut and her breathing even, upright in her mail like a woman dozing on her feet out of long habit. The keeper sat with his hands folded, sometimes watching the book, sometimes watching them, and once, very carefully and with two fingers, trimmed a lamp wick that had begun to smoke, the room holding its breath with him until it was done and nothing had changed. The tally stood, the pen stayed down, and the book did not spend.

Wardens close Kettle when it rings; Mordecai walks up under Kestrel's shield; then we seal and lift the book. I'm with that plan." Below, the room itself seemed to know the hour had turned. Mordecai Tally stood, actually stood, for the first time in all their watching, worked the stiffness out of his fingers, and looked to the west-wall hatch. "It goes up before the bell," he said, half to himself and half to them, "and I log it, as I log every one." He lifted the crate first and the retied bag after, and set them into the hatch's dark with the particular care of a man performing a duty older than himself. Then he took up his pen, opened the book to tonight's page, and waited, pen poised, eyes on the hatch, on the road, on the moment the mercy began to walk.

Round 79. Kestrel rose from her rest by the stair, settled her shield on her arm and her hand on her sword-hilt, and took her final post at the foot of the stair, between the keeper's desk and the west-wall hatch, shield toward the dark. She spoke her place aloud so that every living thing in the room knew it. "Keeper. I rested through the third hour, as the hour allowed - my watch is mine again now. Here is where I stand when the bell rings: at the foot of this stair, between your desk and the hatch, shield toward the dark. The road closes when the bell rings. You walk up under my shield, and only then does the book leave its desk - whole, under the seal. I will not break the book. It holds other people's years, and I will not bury them. I will not break the hand. What I hold is the road, and the stair, and you. That is my vow, and my place." Mordecai Tally heard it with his pen poised and nothing held. "Then I am between a shield and a stair," he said, "which is the safest I have been in thirty years of sitting still." A dry pause. "When the bell rings, mistress, do not wait for me to be brave. Take my arm and walk me up, and I will go."

Mara took her own place on the stair-side of the counting-table, leaving the keeper a clear path to Kestrel and keeping both hands free. "I'm ready. Food out first. Then Pell's word that the road is closed, and the keeper clear upstairs. Only then the book." She looked to Nib, then to Kestrel. "If it resists, or you say papers, I stop." Her single declared action was one slow attempt to lift the ledger level, supporting its boards and pages together without folding, tearing or pulling against resistance, and she would carry it toward the stair only if it came freely and the way was clear. The room was set. The paladin stood between the desk and the hatch, the wizard by the book in his armor of force with his ready word, the rogue in the lamplight, the scout on the street above, the wardens at their posts, the seal in the front room, and the mercy in the hatch's dark, waiting to walk.

Round 80. The fourth bell rang out over the night streets, four slow strokes, and the last one was still humming in the air when the mercy began to walk. Nib had timed his rhythm to it, and the light was on him when it happened: for the first time in all his readings the working's even glow RESOLVED, not brighter and not dimmer but directional, a current in the cloth, a thread running from the west-wall hatch upward and away toward Kettle's door. In the hatch's dark the crate and the retied bag began to recede, slowly, at exactly a walking pace, carried by nothing any eye or spell could name. The book's knot did not change; a mercy is not a draw. Nib said all of this quietly and exactly, the way he would have wanted it said. On the street above, Ashen saw the other end of it: Kettle's door opened at the bell, warm light and a small quiet line of people who ate because the road had walked. When the last bowl was given, two plain-clothed wardens took their places at the door, and it closed, and stayed closed. The word went down through the house the way it had come up: the road was closed. No hand came to draw, none that anyone saw.

Kestrel took the keeper's arm. Mordecai Tally set his pen down on the desk, deliberately, like a man laying down arms, and went up the twenty steps under her shield, past Tam and Ilsabet, into the front room where Pell waited with the seal. "It has been an honor," he said to no one in particular, "to be checked." Then it was only Mara and the book. Food out, road closed, keeper clear. She lifted, slow and level, boards and pages supported together, and it came free, heavy and awkward and willing, and the counting room said one last count in its too-steady voice, the only new count it had offered all night: "Eleven thousand four hundred and six - and three - and one book, carried out." As the book crossed the threshold of its room, the working ended. In Nib's sight the light collapsed, not snuffed but LET GO, ten thousand threads releasing at once and running outward like a held breath finally spent. The saturation was gone, the room was a room, and the far dark was only dark. Upstairs, the keeper stopped dead on the top step and said, in a voice thirty years unused to it, "It has stopped. I can hear it stop." The seal went on the door, the book lay whole in its case under the seal, and the tally was ended.

Bria's Bedside

The lamps are low and the morning has not yet found the windows. A girl lies still and composed, sleeping the sleep that does not spend, and the two rescued men mend in the next room under the same low lamps.

Round 81. Nib said only, softly, "Let us go and see." He went up the twenty steps with the others, watched the seal go on the door and the book lie whole under it, thanked Pell and the wardens, and walked with Kestrel, Mara and Ashen across the waking city back to Pell's House as fast as dignity allowed, and not one of them complained. Sister Ansa was already awake, for wardens sleep badly when the night is strange, and archivists worse. Bria lay where she had lain, and the two rescued men mended in the next room. Nib sat by her, opened the book of his own craft, and gave the reading its ten minutes, careful as he had been careful all night. (Detect Magic, ritual: no slot spent.) Then he told them exactly what he saw and exactly what he did not. He saw nothing: no aura on the girl and none on the men. Whatever of the working had clung to them had let go entirely, the way the room's light had let go, released and not torn. What he could not see, he said plainly: whether a year re-roots when it comes home, whether she wakes, and what "mending strangely" would mean in her case. The spell reported on magic; it could not report on a person.

While they were still sitting with that honesty, Bria did the first new thing she had done since the collector took her year: she moved. It was a small thing, her fingers closing once against the blanket, the way any sleeper's might, and her breathing deepened and her face took color it had not had, and Ansa was out of her chair with her fingers on the girl's wrist before anyone spoke. "Sleep," the old woman said at last, and her voice was not quite steady. "Ordinary sleep. The other thing is gone." She looked up at them, and for once the archivist had no citation. "She will wake when sleep is done with her. Today, if the old cases are any guide - and they are only a guide. Stay close. Be what she wakes to."

Round 82. Kestrel took her place beside Bria's bed with her shield set within reach and her sword-hilt under her hand. She spoke low, not knowing whether the girl could hear her: "Bria. The counting is done. The book is shut and sealed, and the year that was taken is on its way home. Sleep as long as sleep needs you. I will be here when you wake." To Sister Ansa she added, steady and plain, "I will hold this watch until she wakes, however long the day runs. If she stirs, or if anything in this house changes, I am here." She did not touch the girl, wake her or pray over her; she only kept the watch, eyes on the door and the bed in turn. Ansa's answer was not thanks. She dragged the second chair an inch closer to the bed with her foot, set a cup of water on the table between them, and sat back down in her own, one watcher become two, the way a house says yes. The morning passed over Pell's House the way morning passes over a sickroom anywhere, with low light moving across the floor, the breathing of sleepers, and a warden's tread in the hall. And at the promise, or the name, or simply because her time had come round, Bria's breath caught, once. Her fingers closed against the blanket again, and this time they did not let go. Her eyelids fluttered, and beneath them her eyes moved, quick and searching, the way eyes move at the very surface of sleep. Ansa leaned forward with both hands on the arms of her chair. "Easy," she said, low, to the room and not to anyone in it. "Easy. Let her come up herself." Round 83. She came up slowly, the way Ansa had said to let her: a long breath that shuddered at the bottom, a frown that meant dreaming, and then Bria opened her eyes. The first thing she saw was a paladin, armor and shield within reach and a stranger's steady face, and behind her an old woman halfway out of a chair. For a moment the girl only stared, and the room held its breath with her. "...You're not Sister Ansa," she said at last, hoarse, and then caught sight of Ansa behind and looked desperately relieved. "You're - I was at the stall. It was raining." Her hand went to her own face, feeling for something she could not name. "It was raining, and then it wasn't. How long - " She stopped, looked at the window and the morning light, and her voice went very small. "How long was it raining?" Ansa took the girl's hand in both of hers, and for once in her long careful life the archivist did not reach for a ledger. "Long enough," she said gently. "And it's over, and you are home in your own year. These people saw to it." A beat. "They're the sort who'd want you to ask them yourself." Bria looked back at Kestrel, taking in the shield, the hilt under her hand, and the fact that an armed stranger had plainly been sitting guard over her sleep. The girl was fourteen, and frightened, and brave in the way fourteen sometimes is. "Did you catch him?" she whispered. "The man with the pen?"

Round 84. Mara came quietly to Bria's doorway and stayed back from the bed, giving Ansa and Kestrel room. "The keeper from the cellar is with Brother Pell and the wardens," she said. "We brought his book out without tearing a page. The counting there has stopped." Her eyes went to the cup beside the bed. "Would you like some water?" She waited for Bria's answer rather than reaching for her or asking for her story. Bria looked at the stranger in the doorway, dark and quiet and careful to stay back, and something in the care itself seemed to tell her what kind of morning this was. "With Brother Pell," she repeated, knowing the name the way everyone on that side of the hill knew it, and her shoulders came down from around her ears. "Then he's caught. He's actually - " She stopped, and swallowed, and nodded to herself, the way a person does when she has decided to believe good news rather than wait for it to be taken back. "The counting's stopped. Yes. All right." The question about the water earned Mara the first real look, not a frightened patient measuring a stranger but a girl who had been asleep far too long. "Please," Bria said, and her voice cracked on the single syllable, and she laughed at it, a short embarrassed sound. "My throat feels like I've slept through a whole - " She stopped again. Her smile went somewhere complicated. "I did, didn't I. How long was it raining?"

Ansa brought the cup herself and held it while the girl drank, both her old hands around Bria's young ones on the clay. Nobody in the room answered the question yet; it was not a cruel silence but the kind people leave when they want the answer to come gently. "There were others," Bria said suddenly, over the cup's rim. "In my head, while - I think I remember others. A man who cried all the time. Is that real, or was that the rain?"
