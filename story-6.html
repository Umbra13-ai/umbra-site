Pell's Common Room

Grey daylight comes through the shutters of a house that has been awake all night. Bread sits on the table in heels and crusts, the lamps burn low, and the chair where the file lay is empty now. Somewhere above the rooftops, the city is waiting for its second bell.

Round 109. Kestrel did not follow Pell into the street. She kept her place at the table, shield on her arm, and took up the bread Pell had left, eating slowly while her eyes stayed on the door and the shuttered street. After a time she spoke low, to Nib and to Mara and to no one else. "The file is gone with her, and the watch at this table is done. Now the house is ours to hold until she comes back or sends word. Eat. Rest what you can, and I will keep the door." Mara took some of Pell's bread and settled at the table, close enough to hear the door but leaving Kestrel the watch. "I'll eat and rest a little. Wake me if Pell returns or anything changes." Nib ate a heel of the bread slowly on the bench, then spoke low to them both. "Pell said eat and rest, and she is right. Mara, sleep while you can. Kestrel, you have held the table all night; trade me the door for an hour when you want it. I will keep my slots and my spells for when Pell comes back, and I will want Mage Armor fresh before we go anywhere near the Tenth Bell." He left his folded hands where they were, one eye on the latch, and rested without sleeping.

The pale hour stretched. Bread and low words and the latch staying still, and then, bronze over the rooftops again, the second bell. Pell's step came on the stair a little after it, unhurried. She shed the gray wrap, hung it, and stood a moment warming her hands at the lamp before she spoke. "It is filed, and it is moving. The counter opened at first bell as it always does; I waited out one merchant's quarrel over a bill of lading and then it was my turn. My superior read the certificate and the archivist's page where I stood - read them twice - and asked me three questions and no more: whose oath, whose hand, and who else has seen it. I answered all three. The file went into the reader's hands with a docket mark, and the second seal is asked for and under weighing. These things walk on House legs, as I said they would - days, not hours - but they walk, and the first step is taken."

She took the bread's end for herself and sat at last. "No eye lingered on me in the street, and the counter was only a counter. The broom-man is back at his corner across the way, sweeping last night's nothing. An ordinary morning, which is the best kind we get." She looked around the table, at the bread gone to heels and crusts and at three faces short on sleep. "You kept my house. Now tell me what you mean to do with your day, and I will tell you what mine needs."

Round 110. Kestrel answered plainly. The file was filed and the seal walked on House legs; there was nothing to chase at the counter. "Then my day is the one you set for us. I will keep the door and the house while Nib and Mara rest, and I will sit with Bria a while to see she sleeps easy under her wardens. The seal walks on House legs - I will not go rattling the counter for an answer that is not due. If your day needs something of us - an errand, a pair of eyes in the street, a hand at the house - name it and it is done. Otherwise we rest, we watch, and we wait for your superior's word." She settled back into her post by the door.

Pell turned the bread crust in her fingers, considering, and then shook her head once. "What my day needs is exactly what you are doing - this house held, that child sleeping easy, and no new ripples on the water while the seal is weighed. My own day is ink: I owe my House my own account of the filing, written plain and in my hand, while the morning is fresh in me. That is a table-and-lamp task, and I am glad of the quiet for it." She gathered her papers and paused. "If any of you stir abroad today, keep it ordinary - market, chapel, a walk for the air of it. Nothing near the counter, nothing down Kettle-lane. The file walks on House legs now, and the kindest thing we can do for it is to be dull. And Kestrel - when you sit with Bria, tell the wardens I asked after her. The girl mends strangely, as they say, but she mends." She took her lamp to the far corner, and the scratch of her pen began again.

Round 111. Mara finished her bread and lay down to rest a while. "After I sleep, I can take the door so Kestrel can sit with Bria, if she wants. I'll keep away from the counter and Kettle-lane." Nib stood, thanked Pell for the plain instruction, and kept his voice low so as not to break her work. "Dull it is, Mother Pell. I will stay inside the House today." He added one request, and said it was hers to refuse. "When your ink allows, may I have my spellbook back from your keeping? Not to open anything sealed, only to prepare my spells properly for tomorrow, in this room, under your eye or a warden's. If the House would rather it stayed shut until the seal answers, I will understand." Then he sat again by the lamp and left the door to Mara.

Pell's pen stopped. She looked at Nib over the lamp for a long breath, and what she was weighing showed plain on her face: not doubt of him, but habit, the keeper's habit of saying no first. "It is yours, and you ask for it like one who knows what keeping means. Yes." She lifted two fingers toward the warden at the stair, and the woman went and came back with the spellbook wrapped in its plain cloth, the House's cord still knotted around the sealed packet that rode with it. Pell set it on the table between them. "Two conditions, and they are light ones. It does not leave this room until the seal answers. And the corded packet stays corded - what is sealed is sealed, and none of us opens it." She waited for his nod, then pushed the lamp an inch closer to him, which was as close as Pell came to a blessing. "Prepare what you need. Slowly. You have the day." She returned to her ink, and the room settled into its new quiet: pen, pages, and a wizard turning the familiar leaves of his own book under a warden's easy eye.

Round 112. Mara rose from her rest and took the door Nib had left, staying inside the House as Pell asked. "I have the latch. Keep to your book, Nib; I will call if anyone comes." Nib took the wrapped book in both hands and nodded to Pell. "Both conditions kept, Mother Pell. It stays in this room, and the corded packet stays corded." He unwrapped only the book, turned the leaves under the lamp, and went through his prepared list slowly, spell by spell, to be sure of each one. He changed nothing, and said so quietly: "I know what I carry. I only wanted to hold it again." When he was done he wrapped it as it had come and set it back within Pell's reach, the cord untouched. Kestrel spoke quietly, so as not to break Pell's ink or Nib's leaves. "Mother Pell, your trust is well placed - Nib keeps both conditions, and I will see the room kept quiet for his work." She looked to Mara at the door. "Mara, I take your offer. You have the door; I will sit with Bria." She went to the chirurgeon's room, greeted the two wardens, and took the chair beside Bria's bed, shield on her arm and sword sheathed.

The chirurgeon's room was dim and warm and smelled of clean linen and willow-bark tea. The two wardens gave Kestrel the nod of people glad to be relieved of the watching, and one slipped out to stretch her legs while the other took the stool by the door. Bria slept. That was still the shape of it: the deep, even sleep she had kept since she came up out of the counting, her breathing slow and regular as tide. But where in the first days her face had been slack as wax, now there was life under it. Her fingers stirred once against the blanket, finding the edge of it the way sleeping children do, and her brow moved through small expressions, faint as weather passing over water. The chirurgeon's notes on the side table said the same in a clerk's hand: takes broth waking, sleeps again, color better each morning. She mended strangely, as the old woman had said the taken do. But she mended. The house was quiet around her, and the morning held.

Round 113. Nib asked Pell, low, for one plain sheet and a pen from the House's own supply, and sat with them by the lamp, the wrapped book beside him and the cord untouched. On the sheet he wrote, in his small careful hand, only what the table had learned and agreed, in order: the hands match, on the archivist's oath; the name Proctor Merrick Hale; the Tenth Bell Office; the filing carried at first bell; the hold open to any voice until it left. Under it went one line: what moves Hale is not yet known, and the Eye is owed that telling first. He folded the sheet and gave it to Pell to keep with the House's records, so that nothing would be lost if a day went badly.

Pell read the sheet through once, pen idle in her hand, and her mouth did the small thing it did when she approved and did not want to make a ceremony of it. "Plainly put and plainly kept - that is how a thing survives a bad day. It goes in the House record beside my own account, under my seal, and it will say what it says whether or not any of us is standing by to say it." She blotted the ink, folded the sheet once, and set her seal to the fold. "And the last line is the truest thing on it. What moves him is the telling the Eye is owed - and it is owed first. See that it gets it."

The Docket Receipt

Round 114. Kestrel rose from the chair beside Bria's bed and stepped to the doorway, where Pell's words could still reach her, and spoke clearly enough for the house to hear. "Mother Pell, I heard you. The telling is owed to the Eye first, and it will get there - I will see to it myself when the hour comes, and no other telling goes before it." She looked back toward the sleeping girl. "Until then, my post is here. Bria sleeps under my watch, and the house stays kept." She returned to the chair. Mara kept her place by the door, one eye on the latch. "Then keep your ink, Mother Pell. I will keep the doorway. Let the account travel to the Eye before it travels anywhere else." Nib inclined his head to Pell. "It will, Mother Pell. The vow was mine before it was anyone's, and I will not let another ear hear it first." He left it there and kept to the lamp and the quiet.

Pell did not look up from her account at once. She finished the line she was on, set the pen in its rest, and only then turned to Nib. "I know whose vow it was, Nib. I was in the room when you made it." She let that stand a moment, not a rebuke, a weight set down gently. "The Eye holds you to the telling of the unmaking, and you have given that telling true. What moves Hale belongs to the same account when you know it - and when you know it, the Eye hears it from you before it hears it from anyone. I have written that order into my own account today, so the House's record and your vow point the same way." She took up her pen again. "That is all the answering a kept vow needs. Keep it as you have been keeping it."

The day did not stand still, even a dull one. The lamp flames came down as the grey daylight strengthened, Pell's account grew by pages, and the house settled into the small sounds of a place being kept. Just past midday there was a knock, the House's own pattern, two and two, and a runner in House gray handed Pell a slip no bigger than a playing card and was gone again into the street. Pell read it, read it again, and set it on the table where all could see: the docket receipt, stamped and initialed. The file was in the reader's hands in fact now, not only in promise. No word on the seal traveled with it, and none was expected yet, but the walk had a first footprint.

In the chirurgeon's room, the quiet broke gently: Bria woke. Not all the way. Her eyes opened partway, unfocused, and found Kestrel's shield-arm beside the bed without alarm. She took a little broth at the warden's spoon and said one thing, small and clear and already half asleep again: "Is it done counting?" The warden told her it was, and she nodded as if that were the whole of the news she wanted, and went back under, easy. Afternoon leaned toward evening. The street outside did its ordinary business; the pieman's cart creaked home, and the broom-man's corner emptied on its daylight schedule. Pell trimmed the lamps early against the dark and said what was plainly true: "The day was well spent. The night is ours to spend too, and nothing spends it for us unless we choose it."

The Night Shares

Round 115. Nib looked at the docket receipt and let out a slow breath. "A first footprint. That is something." He turned to Pell and the table. "Then I would spend tonight the plainest way there is: a proper rest inside these walls, so that my slots and my head are whole when the seal answers. Kestrel, Mara, shall we split the night watch so no one sits it alone? I will take the middle hour if you will have it. And if anyone hears from Ashen, say that her seat is kept and her question waits, with no weight on it." He asked Pell for a pallet in the room where the others slept. Mara offered the first watch. "I will take the first hour. Nib can have the middle as he offered. Kestrel, would you take the last? We can leave Ashen her choice if she wants it." Kestrel answered from the chair beside Bria's bed, low enough not to wake the girl. "Mara, the door is yours, and I am glad of it. Nib, your word stands with mine. The telling goes to the Eye first - no other ear hears it before that room does."

"A pallet in the common room, and glad to give it," Pell said, and had the warden bring down the good blankets, the ones without mend-marks, which in Pell's house was the closest thing to ceremony for guests who had become something more. "Mara takes the first hour, Nib the middle, Kestrel the last. A house that sleeps in turns sleeps sound." She stopped at the foot of the stair and looked back at them, lamplight under her chin. "Ashen's seat stays set and her question stays hers - I will hear it said to her just so, when she comes. Rest now. You have earned a dull night, and I mean to see you get one." The house settled by degrees. Mara's watch was the last lamp trimmed low and the latch-checked door; the street outside went from ordinary evening to ordinary night, one cart, then none. In the chirurgeon's room Bria breathed slow and even, and the warden by the door nodded to Kestrel like one soldier to another. The night began the way the day had ended: kept, and quiet, and theirs.

Round 116. Kestrel answered Pell from the chair, low. "The last watch is mine, Mother Pell, and I will keep it. Mara, Nib - rest well." She leaned her shield where her hand could find it in the dark and let herself rest. Mara took the first watch at the common-room door, checked the latch, kept the lamp low, and listened through her hour. Nib thanked Pell for the good blankets and took the pallet in the common room, the wrapped spellbook back in Pell's keeping where he had agreed it would stay. "Wake me for the middle hour, Mara, or sooner if the latch turns or anyone calls. I will not sleep deep." He lay down in his robe with his focus in his hand.

Ashen took the choice Mara had left her without making a speech of it. She set her pack by the door-side bench, checked the street latch once, the way she checked everything, eyes open and hands where they could be seen, and said, even and quiet and for no one but herself: "I'll stand the last share with Kestrel. Wake me when it turns." She took the pallet Pell's warden brought, close enough to the common room door that opening it would wake her, and lay down in her boots with her blade on the floorboards beside her hand. Whatever question was waiting on her, she let it wait; the night was for standing her share, and she meant to stand it. Kestrel would find her watch-partner already there when the last hour came round: a second set of eyes on the door, awake on the edge of sleep the way some people learn to be.

Round 117. After the quiet first watch, Mara woke Nib gently for the middle hour, as agreed, and went back to her pallet with her boots and gear close. Nib woke at her touch, nodded, and took the door-side bench with the lamp turned low. He saw Ashen asleep in her boots on the pallet by the door, blade beside her hand, and did not wake her. Under his breath, to no one, he said, "Glad you are back." He kept the middle hour at the latch, hands folded, and when it was done he woke Kestrel for the last share. Kestrel rose without a word and took the bench as he left it. She checked the street latch once, the way the house asked, settled with her shield leaning where her hand could find it, nodded to Ashen on her pallet, and kept the last hour in silence.

The night kept its own accounting and found nothing owing. Mara's first hour was a trimmed lamp and a latched door. Nib's middle was the low bench and the street gone wholly quiet. The last hour stood two watchers at the common-room door, Kestrel upright as a posted spear and Ashen beside her, and nothing tested them. Grey came first, then first bell, thin through the shutters. The house woke in its working order: the warden stoking the kitchen fire, Pell down before the water boiled, sleeves rolled, laying out bread and salt and a kettle as though feeding a table of watch-standers were any ordinary morning's work. "No word walks faster than a seal weighs," she said, pouring. "The day is yours. Spend it as you spent the night - on purpose." Bria's door stayed closed, and behind it there was even breathing. Morning stood open.

The Copy-Work

Round 118. Mara took some bread and salt, left Bria's room undisturbed, and said to Pell, "I can help with whatever ordinary work needs doing today. Is there something useful close to home?"

Pell sized Mara up over the kettle and pointed her chin at a wrapped bundle on the counter: a stack of mended account-books, string-tied, ink fresh on the labels. "Two ordinary things, and you can take either. The warden means to carry these up to the House counter before second bell - a body who can read a docket tag and keep a bundle dry is welcome company, and the walk is the length of a thought. Or, if you'd rather stay in arm's reach of the house, there's the market run - salt, lamp oil, and a knotted cord of the chirurgeon's shopping, which wants carrying more than it wants wit." She poured Mara a second cup without asking. "Nothing in either that bites. The biting work is still being weighed upstairs." A beat. "Bria takes her posset at noon, if you're home for it. She asks after the quiet one with the steady hands." She meant Kestrel.

Round 119. Nib looked up from the bench by the lamp. "If the House has ordinary copy-work, Mother Pell, I am a fair hand with figures and labels, and I would rather be useful inside these walls than idle. Mend-books, tally-slips, anything that wants a steady hand and no questions. If not, I will keep the bench and the quiet." He left the market run and the walk to the House counter to the others.

"A steady hand and no questions is half my trade, and I never refuse it twice," Pell said, and it was settled as simply as that. The warden brought a lap-desk to the counter by the good lamp, then a strongbox of the week's tally-slips and three of the mended account-books whose fresh labels wanted fair-copying into the House's own hand. Pell set down ink, two cut quills, and a blotting-shaker of fine sand. "Copy the labels true and the slips plainer. If a figure looks wrong to you, mark it on a spare slip and leave it - do not mend it yourself. The House's numbers are honest, and honest numbers can stand a question; it's mended ones that can't." It was good work for a wizard with ink-stained fingers: quiet, lamplight, the scratch of two quills when the warden took the second book. The morning shaped itself around the table, with Mara choosing her errand, Nib at the counter, Kestrel due at Bria's door come noon, and Ashen asleep late in the room where the watch had been, her boots still on. Ordinary, and earned.

Round 120. Kestrel took bread and salt from Pell with a quiet thanks, left the copy-work to Nib and the errands to Mara, and settled in the chair beside Bria's door with her shield leaning where her hand could find it, listening for the girl's breathing and the latch below. Pell returned her thanks with a warden's brevity: two fingers tapped to the counter's edge, the House's old sign for a debt neither owed nor owed back, plain bread plainly given. The morning found its rhythm. Nib's quill scratched at the counter, and the warden's second quill answered it. Behind the door where Kestrel kept her chair, Bria's breathing came slow and steady, and once, near mid-morning, there was a small sound that might have been turning over in sleep and might have been almost waking, and it settled back to quiet before it decided. The house held its breath the way houses do when someone mending sleeps inside them. Noon was not far off, and the posset was already warm on the back of the stove.

Round 121. Mara chose the House-counter walk. "I can read the docket tag and keep the books dry. I will stay with you and leave the paperwork in your hands." The warden set a porter's pace through the morning streets, bundle high under one arm, and Mara fell in beside her easily. The House counter was all ordinary business that day: runners coming and going, docket tags called out by number, a clerk who stamped without looking up. The mended books were signed for, a receipt-chit traded across the wood, and the walk home was warmer than the walk out, with the sun over the rooflines and the city minding its own affairs.

Noon found the house gathered where it meant to be. The warden carried the posset tray up, and Kestrel was already at Bria's side when her eyes opened, clearer that day and finding the room without fear in it. "You're the quiet one," Bria said, her voice thin but pleased with itself. "Steady hands. I heard you keeping the door all morning. Nobody keeps a door that well unless they mean it." She drank the posset in small sips, watching Kestrel over the rim with the particular interest of the recently mended. "When I'm on my feet I'll thank you properly. Pell says proper thanking wants a whole morning." She slept again before the tray was cold, one hand curled loose on the blanket. Pell, in the doorway, said nothing at all, but she looked at Kestrel a long moment, and whatever she was weighing, she weighed it kindly.

Round 122. Nib spent the afternoon at the bench in Pell's common room on whatever plain copy-work the House set him: tally-slips, labels, a column of figures to check twice. When Mara came in from the House counter he asked her only, quietly, how the street had looked, and when Kestrel came from Bria's room he asked after the girl. He said nothing of the Eye, the Tally or the file to anyone but the three at the table. Kestrel had answered Bria's thanks softly, so as not to wake her: "A whole morning is a fair price, and gladly paid." She met Pell's look in the doorway with a steady nod and no words, and kept the afternoon at Bria's door.

Bria did not wake, but something in her sleep heard it anyway. Her fingers uncurled on the blanket, and she murmured, not quite words, the sound a house makes when it settles into a warmer hour, and her breathing went deeper than it had since the counting stopped. Whatever mended in her mended faster with a kept door outside it. Pell answered Kestrel's nod in kind, warden to warden, and a while later the real answer arrived on a tray: a cup of the good tea, set on the floor within reach of the chair, no words spent on it. The afternoon lengthened. Downstairs Nib's quill worked toward the last of the tally-slips, and the warden began sharpening a pencil that did not need sharpening, the way people do when they are content to be in a room. The house was quiet, and the quiet was kept, and it knew by whom.

Round 123. Mara answered Nib quietly. "The street was ordinary. The warden handed over the mended books, the clerk gave a receipt-chit, and we came straight back. Nothing drew my eye." Nib finished the last of the tally-slips and blotted the quill. "Ordinary is exactly what Pell asked for. Thank you." He carried the finished slips to Pell's table, squared them, and said only that they were done and checked twice.

Pell took the squared stack, turned the top slip to the light, and read three lines with the air of a woman who has caught copyists out before. She found nothing to catch. "Checked twice, and it shows. The House pays its copyists, and it pays for care, not speed - there's a pay-packet on the spike by the door with your mark on it when you want it." She set the slips in the out-tray with unusual gentleness, for her. "You'd make a clerk's clerk, Nib Quillgear, and I mean that as a compliment and not a sentence. The lamp's yours as long as you want it." The evening came on soft outside the shutters, first the gold going out of the light, then the lamplighters in the street. A quiet day at the House was drawing toward its end, and for the first time since the counting, no one at the table seemed to be counting anything.

Round 124. Kestrel looked in on Bria once more before the light went, her breathing slow and easy and her hand still loose on the blanket, and left the door ajar a finger's width so she would hear if Bria woke. She thanked Pell quietly for the day's keeping, then took her place by the lamp in the common room with Nib and Mara. "Thank the house by sleeping in it easy," Pell said, waving off the thanks, but she was pleased and not hiding it well. "As for watches - the doors are barred and the lamps are mine to trim, so no one owes this house an hour. But a body that sleeps better for keeping one is welcome to the bench, and I won't pretend I mind the company at the late tea." She set the kettle on anyway. "Same shares as last night if you like them. Ashen's is kept either way - she stood her hour at the door this morning past, and no one in this house will say she didn't." The evening settled around the common room: Nib by the lamp with his figures done, Mara with her boots off and her errands behind her, Kestrel where her hand could find the shield. Outside, the street did its ordinary evening and then its ordinary night. Second bell came and went, and nothing came with it but the dark.

Round 125. Mara accepted the late tea and let the day's errands rest. "I kept the door last night. Tonight I will trust your barred doors and take a full rest, unless you ask me back up." Nib took a cup of the tea in both hands and thanked Pell for it. "Same shares as last night, if Kestrel and Mara like them. I will take the middle hour again." He drank slowly by the lamp, then took his pallet in the common room, one eye on the latch.

"Then it's settled as it should be - by whoever's holding the teapot," Pell said, and poured all around. "Mara sleeps whole. Nib takes the middle hour. Kestrel keeps the door's company when it suits her. And Ashen -" a nod toward the stairs, where the fourth bed in the house was occupied past all sensible hours, "- stood her watch before most of us were awake, and her share is kept. This house remembers who stood where." The night passed the way the day had: honestly. Nib's middle hour was lamplight and a still street; the bars on the doors did their silent work; and once, in the small hours, Ashen surfaced from sleep at the changing of the house's sounds, heard the quiet kept and nothing wrong in it, and went back down without a word, boots still on. First bell, and grey light, and the kettle already going. It was the second day of the weighing. The morning stood open, and so did everything in it.

The Second Morning

Round 126. Kestrel rose at first bell and looked in on Bria once more, still asleep, then took her place by the door in the common room, ready if the House's answer came and content to wait if it did not. The second morning of the weighing brought something the first had not: Bria woke all the way. Kestrel was there to see it. The girl's eyes opened and stayed open, finding the ceiling, the window and the door, each in turn and without fear. "How many days?" she asked, and her voice was thin but whole, a thing mending along its own grain.

"Two," Kestrel said, and Bria nodded slowly, as if two were a number that fit in her hands. "I dreamed counting," she said. "But it was the good kind. The kind where you know the total." She sat up halfway against the warden's stacked pillows, took broth in both hands, and looked at Kestrel with the directness of someone deciding a thing. "You kept the door again. I could tell by the quiet. Kept doors have a sound." A pause, and the smallest smile. "It's boring, and it's my favorite." Pell appeared in the doorway with the morning's accounts under one arm, saw Bria sitting up, and said only, "Broth's on the House, today and always," which from Pell was a whole speech. The House's answer still weighed somewhere above them, and nothing in the street below was in a hurry about it.

Round 127. Mara paused at the doorway and smiled at Bria. "Good morning, Bria. It is good to see you sitting up." She gave Bria and Kestrel their space and went back to the common room for breakfast. Nib came to the chirurgeon's door with his breakfast bread still in hand and waited to be asked in. "Good morning, Bria. I am the gnome who sat on the other side of your bed." He kept his voice gentle. "I will not ask you anything about the cart or the cup. I only wanted you to hear it plainly from me: the thing that was taking from you is over, and what it took came home. You are in a kept house, and no one will take you from it. If you want anything I can fetch, a book, a letter written, a window opened, you have only to say." He stayed in the doorway, not crowding her.

Bria took the greetings the way she took the broth, in small sips and with both hands. To Mara, a nod over the cup's rim: "You're the one who walked with the warden. She came back whistling. She never whistles." A beat. "So thank you for whatever that was." To Nib she gave a longer look, and then something behind her eyes placed him. "The other side of the bed," she repeated. "You have a counting voice, but you weren't counting. I remember that. It was -" She hunted for the word, gave up, and laughed at herself, a small cracked sound that turned into a real one halfway through. "It was the first thing I heard that wasn't a number."

She shifted against the pillows, considering his offer seriously, the way a person does when kindness is still a little unfamiliar. "The window, then. Just a crack. I want to hear the street be ordinary at me." And then, quietly, not quite a question: "Nib. That's a good name. It sounds like a pen." Pell, passing the door with the linen basket, did not stop, but everyone in the room heard her pace slow all the same.

Round 128. Kestrel crossed to the window and opened it just a crack, as Bria had asked, then settled back by the door. "Good morning. You woke on your own, and the house is still yours - the door stayed shut and the street stayed ordinary, just as you asked to hear it." A pause. "Rest as long as you like. I keep the door; that is my whole task, and it is a good one." The window opened its crack, and the street came in exactly as ordered: a cart's iron wheel on the stones, two voices haggling without heat, a dog with opinions, somebody's bell, somebody's laugh. Ordinary, top to bottom. Bria closed her eyes and listened to all of it like music she had forgotten she liked. "There," she said softly, to no one and to Kestrel. "That's the sound of nobody counting." She smiled without opening her eyes, and the morning went on around the kept house, and the crack in the window let it.

Round 129. Nib heard Bria from the doorway and smiled a little. "Nobody counting. That is a good sound." He stepped back out of her light and returned to the bench by the lamp so she had the room and the morning to herself. Mara looked back from the common room with a small smile. "Pell did the work. I only kept the books dry. I am glad it gave her a reason to whistle." "Keeping books dry is work," Bria called after her, with the dignity of someone who had clearly never kept anything dry in her life and knew it. "I'm keeping a whole blanket dry right now, and it's harder than it looks." From the kitchen came Pell's voice, dry as the books in question: "It's a clean blanket, is what it is, and it wants to stay that way." Bria grinned into her broth. The morning kept being ordinary, and nobody in the house was tired of it yet.

Round 130. Kestrel answered Bria's boast from her post with a small smile. "Then you are doing harder work than I am. The door has only to stay shut; your blanket has to stay dry, and it has a whole morning to try to escape you." She glanced toward the cracked window and the street sounds coming through it. "Keep it dry as long as you like. The street will stay ordinary, and I will stay here - that is my whole task, and it is a good one." "The blanket does escape," Bria said darkly, tugging it back up for the third time that morning. "It's cunning. But I'm cunning-er." She settled against the pillows with the satisfied air of a general who has the field, and added, quieter: "It's a good task, keeping a door. I know it is. That's why I keep noticing yours." Somewhere below, the warden's second quill had stopped pretending the pencil needed sharpening and started on the week's linen tally instead.

Round 131. Mara carried her breakfast dishes to the kitchen and asked Pell quietly, "Is there ordinary work that needs doing while we wait? I can carry a basket or keep a door, whichever helps." She left the sealed papers alone. Nib looked up from the bench with a small smile at Bria's account of her blanket. "Cunning, but you are cunning-er. I will bet on you." Pell dried her hands on her apron and looked Mara over like a woman checking the weather. "Yesterday's errand is still on the hook - the market run never went. Salt, lamp oil, and the chirurgeon's knotted cord, which has more on it today, since someone's eating broth like she means it. Take the good basket." She paused. "And if the cord's writing is strange to you, that's the chirurgeon's hand, not a cipher. Mostly."

Down the hall, Bria had heard Nib's wager and had opinions. "Bet properly, then! I take payment in bread. Good bread, mind - not the healing kind, the eating kind." "There's no betting in a House of weighing," Pell called, not looking up from the dishes, which was how everyone in the house knew the rule had been broken before and would be again. The morning took its shape: a basket, a list, a cracked window, a bet of bread, and the slow ordinary work of a house that had decided to be a home for whoever was in it.

Round 132. Kestrel answered Bria's demand from her post with a small smile. "Then the wager is settled fairly. Good bread, eating bread - I will see the house owes you that much, and I will not let anyone pay you in the healing kind." She glanced toward the kitchen, where Pell had set the market errand. "The basket is Mara's errand and the house's need. My errand is this door, and Bria's blanket, and the street staying ordinary. That is my whole task, and it is a good one." "Eating bread," Bria repeated, with the gravity of a woman sealing a contract before witnesses. "Crust on, soft middle, and if there's a seeded loaf at the market I claim seniority." She nestled down, immensely pleased with herself, and added: "You're all very strange, you know. Most people wait for a door to matter before they keep it." From the kitchen, without looking up from the cord-list, Pell said, "Seeded loaf is extra. And worth it."

The Market

The morning market is all the way awake: fish on ice, greens stacked in pyramids, an oil-seller's brass lamps hanging by their chains. At the bread stall the queue moves like it means it. Nobody here is watching anyone.

Round 133. Mara took Pell's good basket and the chirurgeon's cord-list. "Salt, lamp oil, and a seeded loaf if the market has one. I'll bring the change and a receipt." She set out, keeping the list and basket close and watching the street as she went. Nib smiled from the bench. "A seeded loaf, extra and worth it. Mara, if the market has one, I will pay my share of it from my own two gold." Mara watched the street the way she watched every street, and the street gave her nothing back but itself: no eyes that lingered, no shoulders that turned away too fast. Ordinary, and honestly so.

The chirurgeon's cord-list decoded under the apothecary's patience into a packet of this and a twist of that, the salt came in a cone of rough paper, and the lamp oil was poured into the House's own flask, because Pell's errands came with Pell's habits. The House's name was good at every stall: chalked on slates, totted on strings, change counted back honest into Mara's hand with a receipt-chit for the basket. And at the bread stall, last of the morning's luck, there was one seeded loaf left, crust dark, seeds like small coins. The baker wrapped it twice when she heard whose table it was for, because Pell's house was known there, and said, "For the girl that's mending. Tell her the crust is the best part and anyone who says different is selling something." Mara walked home with the basket riding light on her hip, the list done, the change and chit in her fist, and the loaf warm through the paper.

Round 134. Kestrel told Bria what the street had already said. "The errand is done. Salt, lamp oil, and one seeded loaf left at the bread stall - the baker wrapped it twice when she heard whose table it was for, and said the crust is the best part." She listened toward the front of the house. "She is walking home with it now, warm through the paper. Your wager will be settled when the loaf is on the table, not before - I will not spend it early."

The street door opened, and the house smelled the loaf before it saw it. Mara set the basket down, the change and receipt-chit squared on the counter for Pell, and carried the twice-wrapped loaf up herself, because some deliveries are too good to hand off. Bria received it in both hands like a reliquary. "Seniority," she reminded everyone, and took the crust end without shame. The first bite took a long time, and was clearly the best thing that had ever happened to her. "The baker was RIGHT," she said, muffled, scandalized that anyone could be so right. Pell cut the rest fair: quarters, a heel for the warden, and a thin slice set aside on a plate that no one had to be told was Ashen's, for when she woke. When Nib put his two gold on the table for his share, Pell pushed one back across the wood with one finger. "The House takes one. The other buys you the next foolish kindness. That's the exchange rate in this house, and it's fixed." Late morning settled in over the crumbs. The loaf was gone, the wager was paid, and for once in its life the whole house seemed to be exactly where it wanted to be.

Round 135. Nib took the returned gold piece and bowed his head to Pell. "A fixed rate, and a fair one. I will keep it for the next foolish kindness, Mother Pell, and I suspect it will not be long in coming." He ate his quarter of the loaf slowly and left the crust end of his share for Bria, who had earned it by seniority. "It won't be," Pell agreed, about the kindness, and let him keep the crust-end sentiment without comment, which for Pell was comment enough. Bria accepted the second crust end with both hands and the solemnity of a duchy changing hands. "This is the best house I've ever been sick in," she announced to the ceiling, and the morning, having no answer to that, just went on being good.

Round 136. Kestrel listened to the house settle after the meal, stepped once to the front to look out at Seer's Row, and came back to the door. To Bria she said softly, "The loaf is eaten, the house is quiet, and the street has nothing to say. Sleep when it comes, and I will be here when you wake. That is the whole of my watch, and it is enough." The house answered quiet for quiet. Bria slept on, bread-happy and mending; Seer's Row went about its late morning with its collars up and its business its own; and the door Kestrel kept stayed exactly as kept as it had been. It was enough. The whole house said so, in the only language houses speak, by holding.

A Hundred Miles

Round 137. Nib kept the bench by the lamp through the quiet late morning, hands folded. When Kestrel stepped to the front once to listen to the street, he looked up and nodded to her and said nothing. Later she rose again to listen at Bria's doorway and look to the wardens, and as she passed Nib on the bench she said, low, "She sleeps easy. The street has said nothing, the house has said nothing, and I mean to let them keep saying it."

The quiet held all the way to midday, and the house stopped pretending it was not resting. At the changing of the hours, Bria walked. Not far: from the chirurgeon's room to the common room, one hand on the wall and the other refusing help with cheerful rudeness. But she walked it under her own power, and arrived announcing that she had crossed "approximately a hundred miles." Pell, coming through with the midday tray, did not correct her arithmetic. Ashen surfaced with the smell of the tray. The slice saved for her was exactly where Pell had left it; she took it with a nod that counted as thanks, ate standing, checked the street latch once out of habit, and claimed a chair by the door like someone who had decided the afternoon could come to her.

Pell set the tray down and looked around the room, at the door kept, the bench warmed, the bread earned. "The House asks nothing of this day," she said. "The seal is still being weighed, and weighing is not watching. Spend the afternoon as you like - and the packet on the spike is still a packet on the spike, Nib Quillgear. It will not take offense either way." The afternoon was open.
