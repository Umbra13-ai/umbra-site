The Marquee

A sign taller than a house, lit against the rain, and under it a door that turns like a wheel and swallows people in gold. Warm air comes out in pulses with smoke and spice in it.

Round 192. The sign over the door read Fortune's Wheel, and well-dressed locals laughed arm in arm as they pushed through the revolving door into the gaming hall. Farrow stopped at the edge of the light and let out a long breath, the first easy one since the bakehouse. "This is the place." The person Farrow worked for owned the house, which meant the peacekeepers did not, and for one evening that was the same as being safe. Mara walked up from the alley to join them and asked quietly, "So your patron owns the Wheel. Who are we going to meet?"

Farrow glanced up at the sign and seemed to decide that the rain had done enough for secrecy. The name was Shemeshka, Shemeshka the Marauder, though nobody said the second part where she could hear it. An arcanaloth, a fox-faced fiend, wealthy past the point where wealth mattered, who now traded in what she knew about who had done what in Sigil, and who kept a web of spies the factions feared. Farrow had not named her in the street because her reputation did its own talking, and they had wanted the party walking toward her door. She had been watching them longer than Farrow had, and she believed she could help with what had been done to them; the rest was hers to say. She did not hurt her guests. They usually left owing her, but they left whole, and better informed than they had arrived.

Nib kept his voice low under the marquee. "An arcanaloth who sells what she knows. Then I would like to hear it plainly, Farrow: what does she expect of guests in her house, and what does she ask in return for hosting us? I will not agree to anything until the others have heard it too." Farrow gave it plainly. She expected guests to be guests: eat, drink, gamble if it amused them, tip the staff, not bleed on the carpets, and not start anything in her house they would have to finish in it. The Wheel was neutral ground and she kept it so the hard way. For hosting she asked nothing tonight; they were expected, and she received people she had waited for the way others staged weddings. Anything more she would ask in person, with the terms on the table, and they could hear it and say no. People did say no to her and walked out, and she remembered that they had. Walking through the door agreed to exactly one thing: dinner. Kestrel nodded once. "Then dinner it is. I walk in as a guest of this house, and a guest I will be. I eat at her table, I keep her rules, and I agree to nothing I have not heard from her own mouth."

The Wheel

Dice rattle under a chandelier of drifting glass globes, a quartet plays something almost familiar, and a clock built like an astrolabe tells the hour over the cashiers' cage.

Round 193. The revolving door took Kestrel out of the rain and into warmth, light and music. From inside, the Wheel looked less like a casino than a very successful theater that happened to take bets: green felt and brass cages, a chandelier of slow glass globes, a bariaur in a silk jacket arguing odds with a tiefling croupier, a githzerai drinking alone, two humans in clothes worth more than a farm laughing at the dice. Eyes noticed the armed, road-worn newcomers and politely slid away; neutral ground had manners. Before she had gone three steps a young tiefling bellhop in burgundy intercepted her and bowed to the company as a whole. He was Valek, and he said the proprietor had been expecting them. He offered a sealed envelope on a small tray, and said that suites had been prepared upstairs at no charge, and that meals and drinks at the tables were on the house tonight. The letter was short and in an elegant hand: rest, refresh yourselves, enjoy my house, I look forward to making your acquaintance this evening. It was signed with a small sigil of an arcanaloth's profile worked into a knot of lines.

Mara thanked Farrow for the road and said that she would hear Shemeshka out, because any bargain could wait until she knew its price. She went in through the revolving door, the Cakers' slice still wrapped and uneaten, and stopped just inside to find Kestrel. Farrow tipped her two fingers off the brow and said that their commission ended at the door and the house would take care of them now, and that in her house they would do well to take the rest. Then the door kept turning and Farrow was gone into the rain. Nib thanked Farrow with a small bow before he stepped through, and stopped just inside beside the others, taking the room in without taking the tray or claiming a suite.

All three chose the same thing. Kestrel gave her plain thanks, laid a silver piece on Valek's tray and said she would take the suite and the rest that came with it, and that word should reach her wherever she was in the house when the proprietor arrived. Mara said, "I am going to sleep while the house is offering a bed. Take your own time; I will see you when she arrives." Nib said, "A suite, please, and word when the proprietor arrives. I would take the long rest like the others." Each was shown to a third-floor suite with a canopied bed, a steaming copper bath, tall windows over rain-washed rooftops and fine clothes in the wardrobe with the house's compliments, which all three left hanging. It was the first real bed any of them had seen since day six.

Round 194. Evening came to the Lady's Ward quietly, and early, and lit by other people's money, and the rest did its work. Kestrel woke as the light turned toward evening, said a short prayer of thanks, and put her own armor back on without going down, since the house had promised word. Mara woke in her suite and knocked lightly at Kestrel's door. "It is Mara. I am awake. Shall we meet downstairs when Nib is ready?" Nib woke, dressed in his own clothes, went over his spellbook as he did every morning, and stepped into the corridor. "I am up. Shall we go down together and see whether the proprietor has come?" The word came before they finished deciding to go looking for it. Valek appeared at the landing, polished and composed. The proprietor had arrived and was taking her table in the mezzanine, and asked that her guests join her at their leisure. There was no urgency; she would hold court all evening. Below, a ripple went through the casino's noise, heads turning like wheat in the wind.

Shemeshka's Table

The mezzanine hums at a cultured pitch above the floor, and from its best table you can see everything else. The woman who owns the house does not look up until she chooses to.

Round 195. Kestrel took the invitation and went up first. Valek led her not back down but up, by a narrower stair off the gallery, where the noise of the floor fell to a hum. Shemeshka sat alone at the best table, utterly at ease: tall and vulpine, rust-red fur groomed to a gloss, dressed in layered indigo silk that cost more than most ships, one clawed hand near a glass of amber and the other holding a slim bound ledger, which she closed as Kestrel approached. Gold eyes, bright with intelligence and appetite in equal measure, took in the armored paladin with approval. She called Kestrel one of her guests and the soldier of the company, gestured to the chairs, and said that in her house a guest's no was honored as much as their yes. Her companions were welcome whenever they chose; some conversations were better had whole, and she was in no hurry. Nib followed up the stair and took a seat beside Kestrel without taking any wine. "Madam, thank you for the rooms. I would like to hear what you have to say before I say anything of my own." She called him the scholar, and a careful one; a guest who listened before he spoke honored his host twice. What she had to say beyond hospitality she would say to all three at once, because she disliked repeating herself, and the friend with the appetite for cakes was not at the table yet. Kestrel said she would not ask her to repeat herself and would wait for Mara.

Mara came up the stair a little later, gave the host a small nod, and took an open chair near the others. "Thank you for the room. I am Mara. I would like to hear what you wanted to tell all of us." Nib moved his chair a little to make room. "We are all here now, madam. I am listening." Shemeshka set down her glass with the precision of a woman marking the real start of an evening, and said it once, plainly, because they had earned plain.

They had woken in the Mortuary with their names in their mouths and nothing behind them: no past, no people, no reason. Someone had made them empty, and not by accident. She opened the ledger, turned it, and slid it to the middle of the table, the page blank and waiting. Finding out who they had been, who had emptied them and why was work, the kind she did better than anyone in the city, and nothing true was free. Her price was one favor, of a kind within their power, named when she needed it and not before. In return she would put her people, her library and her long memory to work on their pasts, and tell them everything she found. If they refused, they stayed her guests tonight regardless, with rooms, board and her protection; hospitality was not leverage, whatever the Cakers had taught them.

Round 196. Kestrel listened through to the end, with the ledger's blank page before her. "Madam, I thank you for saying it once and saying it plain. I want to know who I was, who emptied me, and why. That wanting is real, and I will not pretend otherwise. But a favor you will name when you have need of it and not before is a door I cannot walk through blind, and I will not speak for my companions; each of us must answer for ourselves. So before any of us agrees to anything, I would know the bounds of it: will this favor ever ask me to break my oath, to harm the innocent, or to turn on those I stand beside? And when you do name it, will I have the right to refuse if it asks what I cannot give?" Shemeshka took the questions the way a jeweler takes a loupe, as a compliment to the merchandise, and answered on three fingers. She did not deal in broken oaths, since a paladin who could be made to forswear was worthless to her afterward, and whatever she named would not ask them to betray their god, their oath or the people at that table. She did not set contractors on the innocent; the work might be dangerous, but it would be the kind of work the company was suited for, within its power, and not an errand anyone's cousin could run. The third finger had the edge back in it. There was no refusal once the favor was named; a favor that could simply be declined was a gift, and she was no charity. That was exactly why she bound herself on the first two points now, in front of witnesses, before any line went into the ledger. Everything else was honest work they could refuse tonight, before they owed it.

Round 197. Mara kept her hands off the ledger and watched Shemeshka as she spoke. "A favor I cannot refuse later deserves care now. You said you will tell us all you find about our pasts. Does that hold even if what you find points toward you or someone whose interests you protect?" She asked for an Insight check on Shemeshka's candor. (Insight 16 + 2 = 18: a strong read. Shemeshka believed every word of her pitch, including the claim that she was unafraid of the trail; the unnamed favor was an openly admitted unknown, priced as one, and not a trap hidden in her manner. An 18 could not read a favor she had not yet chosen.) Shemeshka gave Mara a long, approving look. Everything she found, they would get. And if the trail ran toward her or hers, she would say so to their faces and keep talking, because a secret she could not sell about herself was a chain around her own neck, and she wore no chains.

Nib spoke quietly. "Madam, you have been plain, and I thank you. One more question, then. Before any of us writes a line in your ledger, will you tell us what the favor is, or at least how large it is and how long we would have to do it? I will not agree to something I cannot measure, and I will not decide for the others." She could not name it, she said, because she did not know it yet, and she would not pretend otherwise to close a deal. It would not come due this tenday and likely not this month. When she named it they would have fair time to do it, and it would live inside the bounds she had just given: their oaths, their companions and their conscience were not in play, and it would be work they could actually perform. She was not buying a tool she meant to break on first use. That was as much measure as an unnamed thing could honestly carry, and if it was not enough the answer was no, and dinner was still on the house.

Round 198. Kestrel answered first, for herself alone. "Madam, you told me the shape of the debt, and I have weighed it. My oath, my god, and the people at this table are off the table by your own word, spoken before witnesses. No innocents. Work I can do, with fair time to do it, and not soon. I asked for the bounds because I will not give my word blind, but I will give it when the terms are honest, and these are. So here is my answer, mine only: yes. Put my line in your ledger. One favor, owed when you name it, inside the bounds you just gave." Shemeshka took up a silver pen and wrote Kestrel Vow's name in three short strokes and one long one, and the ink gleamed a moment and dried and was real. A name, a date, one favor, fairly bounded. The work began that hour, she said. She did not promise answers quickly, because the truth about an emptied person had been buried by whoever emptied them, and burials took digging. When she knew something, they would know it.

Nib asked what she offered a wizard, for himself alone, and said he would give his own answer before the night was out. She did not tier her bargains, she said; unequal deals made unequal enemies. But he collected what was true, and she was offering the truest thing he would ever own, his own first chapters, and clients in good standing had been known to be allowed reading privileges in the largest private library of recorded secrets on the planes.

Mara spoke next. "I want the truth of who I was and who took it from me. You have answered the question I needed answered. Yes, for myself: I owe one favor when you name it, within the bounds you gave us before witnesses. In return, find what happened to me and tell me all of it, even if the trail leads to your own door." The silver pen came out again, and Mara Venn's name went into the ledger beneath Kestrel's. If Shemeshka's own name was written anywhere in Mara's missing past, she said, Mara would read it from her hand. Two lines, and the ledger closed gently.

Nib closed his book. "I have heard the terms, madam, and they are fair. For myself, I will not write my name tonight. A debt I cannot measure is one I would not carry well, and I would rather say so than say yes to be polite. I mean no insult to Mara or Kestrel, who chose for themselves. If you ever sell what you know for a price I can count, I would hear it. I remain your guest, and I thank you for the rooms and the table." There was not a flicker of offense in the gold eyes. A clean no, plainly spoken, with reasons, was rare at that table. He had no line tonight and remained a guest exactly as long as he chose, with the rooms, the table and the house's protection never having been contingent. The price he could count was noted and filed; her stock changed, and she never forgot a customer who knew what he would rather buy.

Round 199. Kestrel took up the board of small, excellent things and a freshened glass, and ate with a soldier's unhurried appetite, the first easy meal in days. "To choices made cleanly, each our own. Mara, I am glad the ledger holds your name beside mine, and gladder still it holds it on your terms. Nib, a clean no is its own kind of courage, and I respect it." Mara raised a glass to them both. "To choosing our own doors." After days of trail rations and tea nobody drank, hot, excellent cooking landed almost like a spell. Kestrel kept her watch without show and it reported honestly: the Wheel's security was everywhere and invisible, servers materialized before glasses emptied, a loud dice table was gently re-seated, and a drunk who reached for someone else's winnings vanished backward into the crowd between one blink and the next, still holding his own chips. Nobody watched the table with anything sharper than envy; to be seen at Shemeshka's table was itself a spectacle. A githyanki in formal dueling silks lost magnificently at the far table and applauded the house. (A passive watch; no roll.)

Round 200. Mara kept her voice low. She still had the slice from the Cakers and had never trusted it enough to eat it, and she asked Nib to look it over with her before she decided what to do with it. "Gladly. Keep it in your hand, I do not need to touch it." With her leave, he cast Detect Magic as a ritual, taking the extra ten minutes and no slot, while Shemeshka watched with professional interest and did not interrupt. The slice was magical. A faint aura, cold and patient, clung to it, necromancy, threaded through the pastry like dye through cloth: a curse, dormant, embedded, waiting. Nothing in it touched Mara, who had carried it all along without effect. Kestrel set down her glass at once. "Mara, keep it wrapped, and keep it in your hand. Nothing of it goes in a mouth, and nothing of it touches the table's food." She shifted to the rail with her shield on her arm, aided Nib's closer reading with the Help action, and became the wall between the work and the room.

Nib steadied his breath and examined the wings and the glossy veins with an Arcana check, hands off, with the advantage of her help. (Arcana d20 1 and 4, take 4, + 8 = 12 vs DC 16: failure.) It was no wizard's craft and no temple curse he could name, with no signature. The dark veins looked wet, more like blood than chocolate, and the wings were shaped too truly for any bird or bat he knew. Shemeshka, who had watched the whole thing over the rim of her glass, set it down. "A present from the Cakers." She would give one thing free as a housewarming, for the entertainment: nothing that troop baked was ever only cake. As host, she advised that whatever he held, he should stop holding it. If they wanted to know exactly what it was, she could find out, but that was the sort of thing she sold, and they had all had quite enough of her prices for one evening.

Round 201. Kestrel did not argue with either the failure or the advice. She turned to Mara and held out her hand, palm up. "Mara, let me carry it now. The reading is done, and no hand needs to hold it any longer." She took it only if Mara gave it. Shemeshka observed that it was prudent, and added that the answer would not have been charged to a guest mid-meal; she had standards about poisoning her own guests, even secondhand. "Whatever you do with it, do it outside my carpet." Mara extended the slice, still wrapped. "All right. Keep it sealed and away from the food. We can deal with it outside the house." The bundle changed hands without being opened. Kestrel wrapped it in a second cloth, tied it off neat as a field kit, and buried it deep in her pack, away from the food and from any idle fingers, where dangerous things ride, accounted for. The last piece of Undersigil stopped following the company around the table.

Nib nodded to her. "Thank you. That is the safest place for it. I read it poorly and I will say so. If no one needs me, I would like to go up, rest, and go over my book. We can decide in the morning how to deal with it outside the house." Shemeshka laughed, low and genuine. He had read it well enough to keep it out of everyone's mouth, which was the only reading that mattered before midnight, and the digging she owed his companions went on while he slept. Valek was at his elbow before he had fully risen. But Nib held his place, because his pause was for his companions' word. Kestrel gave hers plainly. "Go up, Nib. You read it well enough to keep it out of everyone's mouth, and that was the reading that mattered. Rest, go over your book; the morning will still be there, and so will we." Then he looked at Mara. "Mara, I will go up when you are ready for me to. If you would rather I stay a while longer, say so and I will."

Round 202. Mara did not answer. The Wheel ticked another lazy notch, glasses were refreshed, nobody's evening was billed for waiting, and the hours went by without her voice. Kestrel stayed beside her and pressed nothing. She said only that the night was theirs and there was no hurry in it, and that she would sit with Mara or walk her up whenever she liked. The crowd on the floor thinned by ones and twos, winners carrying their luck out before it cooled and losers leaving before theirs got ideas, and Valek came by once with fresh candles for the mezzanine lamps and left without a word.

In the end Nib waited a little longer and then rose. "Mara has her own pace and I will not press her. Kestrel, you have given your word and that is enough for me. If Mara wants me, she knows where my door is." He gave the house a small bow, which Shemeshka acknowledged across the mezzanine with two fingers lifted from her glass, neither keeping him nor speeding him. Valek walked him up a half-step behind, asking nothing, and the suite was lamplit and quiet, the good desk by the window, the skyline of Sigil doing its slow gymnastics beyond the glass. He kept the key, took nothing from the house, sat by the lamp with his book as he did every night, and took his long rest with his boots by the bed. No knock came and no bill slid under his door.

Below, the lamps burned lower and the Wheel's ticking thinned toward the small hours. Kestrel let one small thought stand on its own, spoken low and for no one's answer: a prayer for Ashen, wherever the road had taken her, that she walked it in better company than she had left. Then she let it go and settled back beside Mara. Once, without breaking the quiet, she rested a hand on her pack where the sealed slice lay, and lifted it again; the morning's work would keep until morning. The floor went to its skeleton crew, a swept table here, a counted till there, the casino intending to be beautiful again by breakfast. The night asked nothing, and Kestrel kept her watch.
