Ragpicker's Square

A plaza of mud, rag-picker stalls and leaning tenements spreads out under a sky that is no sky at all. Above the rooftops the city curves up on every side, rising like the inside of a great wheel, banded in haze. This is Sigil, the City of Doors, a whole world folded into a ring.

The climb is a cargo winch, a swaying chain-basket and the long, wet throat of the chute, and then light. Despite Pell's warning, all three of them stare. Somewhere below that impossible horizon, they were cargo.

The Gilded Tally

The Market Ward is everything Pell promised: a canyon of counting-houses and stalls, a thousand deals a minute, and no one looking twice at three battered strangers. The Gilded Tally announces itself with a sign of a brass quill over a ledger, a narrow marble front wedged between a money-changer and a pawnshop. Inside is a long counter and a queue of runners with sealed pouches. Clerks weigh coin and write in exactly the clipped money-hand from the pages. A clerk looks up from the counter. "Next. Deposit, withdrawal, or inquiry?"

Round 10. Nib keeps to quiet strangers. He glances at Kestrel and Mara, and they hang back a few steps by the door to watch the queue and the runners while he steps up to the counter alone, pouchless and polite, with Pell's pass-mark hidden in his sleeve. "An inquiry, please." To the clerk, mildly: "A friend of mine is holding a paper bearing your house's brass quill and a counter-sigil, and is unsure it is genuine. How does one go about having a receipt of the Tally verified, and who is permitted to ask?" He says nothing of triangles, the Mortuary or the eleventh bell, and while the clerk answers he tries to read him, and the ledgers behind him, for the clipped money-hand he saw on the pages. Kestrel, a few steps back by the door with Mara, keeps her eyes on the queue and the runners rather than on the clerk's face. The clerk's answer is polite, practiced and absolute. "Verification is done in person, with the document in hand and the account's token or passphrase. Only the depositor, a named beneficiary, or a bearer of the account's token may inquire into an account. To anyone else, the Tally neither confirms nor denies. Discretion IS the service, sir." A beat. "For five gold, however, the house will authenticate a DOCUMENT: paper, seal, and hand certified genuine Tally work, without any word about the account behind it. Many find that sufficient." Nib reads the clerk while he talks and finds nothing; the man's face is a trained ledger, and Nib's own awareness of the letter in his pocket muddies the water. (Insight 2 + 3 = 5: nothing.) Kestrel, watching the room, notes the front door behind them, a curtained arch behind the counter, and a side stair with a house guard posted: two tallymen with cudgels, bored. At the back counter a senior proctor's head came up once at the word "verify," then went back to his books. The money-hand is everywhere and nowhere, a trained house style written identically by half a dozen clerks, so no single hand stands out. (Perception 13.) The clerk waits, pen poised. "Shall I book the authentication, sir? Five gold."

Round 11. Nib considers it for a breath, glances back at Kestrel and Mara by the door, and nods. "Yes, thank you. Authenticate the document, please. Five gold." He counts five gold pieces onto the counter and holds up his folded letter, still sealed by its folds, keeping it in his own hand across the counter. "The paper and the mark of the seal that was once on it, only. It is not to be opened, and I will not give a name. If it cannot be examined unopened, I will take my coin back and leave." The clerk never asks him to let go. A lens, a lamp, a careful minute over the paper and the pale scar along its fold, and then the clerk beckons the senior proctor, who comes with his own lens and a small brass-capped vial. They compare and confer in murmurs, scraping nothing and opening nothing. Then the proctor writes a certificate in the house's clipped money-hand, stamps it with the brass quill and reads it aloud in a flat professional voice. "Paper: Market Ward bond-stock, consistent with house issue, the same family of stock this house cuts for its receipts, though not exclusive to it. Seal residue: brass-flecked wax. Certified: this is the Gilded Tally's OWN house sealing wax. Script: none present." He slides the certificate across. "Whoever sealed that letter, sir, sealed it with this house's wax. What was under the seal, the house cannot say." (Nib pays 5 gp; 10 gp left.) Kestrel, from the door, watches the tallymen by the stair drift two steps nearer the counter when the vial comes out. After the stamping, the proctor jots a small note on a slip, folds it once and passes it to a runner, who leaves through the curtained arch. (Perception 15.) The house sells discretion, but a stranger authenticating the house's own wax is, itself, information. The clerk is already looking past Nib to the queue. "Next."

A Dry Fountain

A quiet courtyard off the edge of the Ward, with a dry fountain and no windows overlooking it.

Round 12. Nib tucks the certificate and the unopened letter into his robe, nods politely to the clerk and proctor, "Thank you, that is all I needed," and walks from the counter without hurry, not looking back. At the door, low and calm, to Kestrel and Mara: "We should not linger here. The house sealed my letter with its own wax, and the proctor sent for someone. Please tell me what you saw as we walk." He asks Mara, who knows how to move through a city unseen, to choose a roundabout way out of the Ward, and Kestrel to walk close behind them. Kestrel falls in at the rear, shield on her arm, and tells him quietly what she saw. "I saw it too. When the vial came out, the tallymen by the stair drifted two steps nearer the counter. After the stamping, the proctor folded a small note and passed it to a runner, who left through the curtained arch behind the counter. Someone in this house now knows the question was asked." They go by side arcades, a cut under a cloth-merchant's awning and a stair that doubles back, Mara's instinct choosing every turn, and Kestrel keeps rear watch the whole way. (Perception 18: nobody follows.) The runner went somewhere, but not after them, not yet. At the dry fountain Nib takes out the folded letter and opens it. Inside is one page of the same bond-stock and two hands. The first is a cramped, careful scholar's script, and Nib stops breathing, because it is his. He remembers writing it: a lamp, a rented desk, the decision to spend coin he did not have. It is a draft of a letter to the Gilded Tally, commissioning them to broker one question to whoever holds the brass triangle's standing order. "What is being collected, and for whom, and what does the collector want with the living?" Below it, in the Tally's clipped money-hand, four lines only: "Received and sealed. Your fee accepted. The answer travels by the usual route; it arrives with the next collection, at the eleventh bell. Present this page." The letter he could not open was his own question, and the Tally's answer is coming through the drop tonight. The page in his hand is the claim ticket.

Round 13. Nib sits down heavily on the rim of the fountain with the page in shaking hands and for a moment says nothing. Then, quietly: "The hand at the top is mine. I remember writing it, a lamp and a rented desk and coin I did not have. I do not remember what I hoped to learn, or why I never broke the seal. But it seems the answer comes to the drop off Ragpicker's Square tonight, and this page is the claim ticket." He holds it out for them both to read. "I would not walk into that blind, and I would not walk in alone. May we go and look at the place well before the bell, find somewhere to watch it from, and decide together whether I present this at all?" He says plainly that he is afraid, but that he wants to know. Kestrel answers, shield on her arm. "Yes. We go and look first, well before the bell, and we decide together whether that page is ever presented. I will not walk you into that drop blind, and I will not let you walk in alone." The page, she adds, stays in his hand, and Mara should pick the route, since she knows these streets. They go the roundabout way, Mara choosing the turns, Kestrel at the rear and Nib between them with the page against his heart.

The Drop

The Square at this hour is itself: rag-pickers striking their stalls, mud, cookfire smoke, and the Mortuary's black bulk shouldering over the rooftops. Against its northeast wall, down a narrow court, is the thing you came to see.

Round 14. The drop is a loading grate set into the Mortuary's upper wall, iron-barred and big enough for a handcart's cargo, with a worn stone ramp where a hundred carts have stood. The chute they climbed that afternoon runs down from it. A cart could come and go in minutes and no one would look twice; the Mortuary takes deliveries at all hours. The court offers four vantages: a tenement stoop across the way, close, exposed and with the best view of the grate; a rag-stall awning twenty paces up the Square, with cover and a partial view; a low roof reachable by a drainpipe, with a full view but slow to leave; and the shadow of the Mortuary wall itself, closest and blind to the Square. Nib studies the court and speaks low. "If you agree, I would watch from the rag-stall awning, with cover and a partial view and an easy way out. Mara, you can judge whether the drainpipe roof is worth it for the full view; it looks slow to leave in a hurry, so only if you are sure. Kestrel beside me under the awning. And I would not stand at the grate, or on the stoop, until we have seen who comes." Two hours early, he quietly casts Mage Armor on himself so it will still hold at the bell. Kestrel moves to the awning, shield on her arm and longsword sheathed but ready. "I'll hold here, under the awning, and keep watch on the court and the Square. I won't go to the grate alone and I won't stir anything before the bell. This is only watching." Mara takes no position of her own and stays close by the awning. Time slows. A pair of Dustman wardens makes one more round, lamplight sliding over the court's mouth, and moves on. Then the eleventh bell counts out across the city, and as the last stroke fades, wheels sound. A handcart turns into the court, pulled by two big silent figures in gray. Behind it walks a third, hooded against the damp, a brass triangle pinned at the throat, gray eyes under the hood and the sharp smell of vinegar on the air. The cart's cargo is three shapes under waxed canvas, rope-bound, and as a porter sets the shafts down one of the shapes moves, a slow, drugged stir and a muffled sound. The collection is people. From within the Mortuary, iron groans as the grate begins to rise. At the collector's belt, beside a brass seal-stamp, hangs a waxed leather pouch tied with house cord.

DM Ruling. Nib's Mage Armor holds, AC 14, with hours to run. His spell slots stand at 1 first-level and 2 second-level; the platform's counter cannot store his Arcane Recovery from the rest, so the table record governs.

Round 15. Nib breathes in sharply at the sight of the stirring shapes and whispers under the awning, "Those are living people, and the grate is already rising. I will go out with the page and claim what I paid for, and ask the collector about them in front of the whole court. While he looks at me, I would be grateful if you could get to the cart and free them. If he turns on me, I will step back toward you, and I will not stand within his reach." Kestrel steps out beside him. "Yes. I will not let you stand before him alone. You keep the page in your hand; it is yours to present, not mine. I will stay at your side, between you and the porters, so you can step back to us if he turns on you." Nib walks out into the lamplight with the page held high, stopping thirty feet from the cart, his reaction ready for Shield. In a clear, steady voice: "I am the one who wrote this. Present this page. I have come for my answer. And before it passes my hands, who are those three, and where are they going?" The collector does not startle. He raises one gray-gloved hand and the porters stop like statues. Something crosses his still face that is not surprise. Recognition. "The dawn collection. You walked. That is the Mortuary's grief, not mine. You were delivered alive and whole, as contracted." A pause. "As were they." He takes the page at arm's length, checks it against a cord-bound list from his pouch, and draws out a sealed packet, thin, waxed leather, house cord, the Tally's brass quill pressed in the wax. "The page is genuine. Your answer, as contracted and paid for." He lets go the moment Nib takes it. To Nib's second question he gives the first thing like an emotion the man has shown, a thin, professional pity. "Not yours. Settled in advance, like you were. Ask the house what your fee bought; it bought one answer, and you are holding it." The porters lift the shafts, the grate stands open, and one of the canvas shapes stirs again, closer now. Kestrel can see it plainly: cutting them free now, in front of two porters and their master, would be a fight under the Mortuary's lamps with wardens a bell's echo away.

DM Ruling. The "that would be a fight" assessment covers one specific move: cutting the captives free in the open, in front of the porters and the collector. It is not the only door. Talking, buying or bargaining, deception, a distraction, calling the wardens' attention to the cargo, following the cart or the pouch, or using the packet or the pass-mark will each be played on its merits, and an NPC's threats are what he would do, not a script. The scene holds for the players' choice. The warden at the grate is checking the manifest line by line and the porters unload one shape at a time.

Round 16. Nib tucks the packet unopened inside his robe. "Thank you. I will not trouble you for the answer." He does not accuse the collector and does not step toward the cart. Instead he turns to the warden checking the manifest at the open grate and holds up Brother Pell's wax pass-mark, his voice clear and meant for the whole court. "Warden, a moment, please. Brother Pell of the House of Dust sent us up with this. By the House's own rule the intake takes the dead. Before you stamp that cargo, will you please check it for breath? At least one of those shapes is speaking. Brother Pell has this tenday's ledger and will want to know before anything goes down the chute." Kestrel steps forward with him, keeping herself between Nib and the porters, and speaks plainly to the warden. "Warden, hear him. By the House's own rule the intake takes the dead, and at least one of those shapes spoke. Check them for breath before you stamp that manifest. If they live, they do not go down the chute tonight." The warden takes the pass-mark, looks at the wax, looks at Nib and at the cart. He does not need persuading; he needs five seconds. (No roll: the evidence checks itself.) He pulls back the canvas and puts two fingers to the nearest throat, then the second, then the third, and stands up slowly, his voice gone hard. "All three breathing. Drugged, by the smell. The House takes the DEAD. This intake is REFUSED." The collector begins, "A misunderstanding of the consignment-" and the warden puts a whistle to his lips and blows three short blasts. Boots answer from inside the grate, two more wardens at a run and a fourth going back in with a message for the lower floors, for Pell. The collector weighs his two porters, four wardens and the House's doorway in one cold moment and chooses the professional's exit from a bad position. He raises both hands. "I am a bonded carrier of the Gilded Tally, operating on settled contracts. This detention will be answered by my house." He does not resist. The wardens cut the ropes, three captives, two men and a woman, slack and mumbling, alive, and carry them inside on litters to the chirurgeon. Nib thanks the warden warmly and asks that the cart and the collector be kept as they are until Brother Pell arrives. (Rescue without bloodshed: 225 XP each. Party at 1,491 XP.)

The Chirurgery

The chirurgeon's room goes quiet the way a room does when everyone in it is listening to something they cannot hear.

Round 17. Kestrel follows the litters a short way inside and kneels beside the nearest captive, the woman, laying one hand on her and speaking low so as not to startle her. "You are safe. You are in the House of Dust, and no one will take you down the chute tonight. Rest." The gift does not answer, but the chirurgeon's care does its slower work: the drug is wearing off on its own, and the woman is surfacing toward real wakefulness. Meanwhile Nib draws the sealed packet from his robe without breaking it and casts Detect Magic on it as a ritual, taking the ten extra minutes and spending no slot. Nothing. No aura on the paper, the cord or the wax; no trap, no spell, no sending-stone whisper. Whatever the Tally sold him, it is only paper. Pell arrives at a near run, robe thrown over his nightshirt, and takes in the court: the refused cart, the cut ropes, the detained carrier, three litters. "You stopped a collection at my grate," he says, and for once the dry voice is not dry. "The House owes you twice over. He" (a look at the side room) "will answer questions. But first I think there is something you have been carrying all night." With Kestrel beside him, Mara near and Pell watching, Nib breaks the Tally's wax and opens the packet. One page, the clipped money-hand, terse as a bill of sale. "Your question was put to the holder of the brass triangle's standing order. The holder answers, conveyed WITHOUT WARRANTY: the collections are the living who will not be missed, delivered breathing and MEMORY-SPENT. The patron pays in old coin through a solicitor of the Clerk's Ward, who signs with an open eye. What the patron wants with them, the holder does not ask. The Tally conveys; it does not verify. Further questions, further fees." Memory-spent. They are all three holding pieces of lives they cannot remember, and now they know the forgetting was not an accident of their collection. It was part of the purchase.

DM Ruling. Kestrel's Lay on Hands pool is spent. She gave the whole pool to Mara in the chute-room fight, and a short rest does not restore it. The platform's counter still reads 15/15, but the table record (0/15 until a long rest) governs.

Round 18. Nib reads the page a second time, quietly, and is silent a long moment before he trusts his voice. He hands it to Pell and offers to let the House copy it, keeping the original. "Brother Pell, it says the collections are people who will not be missed, delivered breathing and memory-spent. Spent on purpose, as part of the sale. That is what happened to the three of us, and I think it has happened to the three on those litters." He asks three things of the House: that the woman, the two men and the dawn scribe be kept under the chirurgeon's care and a warden's eye, that he and Kestrel may question the collector before a warden, and that they may sit with the woman before anyone questions her when she wakes. "I know a little of how she will feel." Kestrel stands at his shoulder. "Brother Pell, I stand behind everything Nib has asked. The woman is surfacing, and I ask to sit with her when she wakes, before any questioning, so she wakes to a friend and not a stranger's questions. And when you question the collector, I will stand at Nib's shoulder as witness, under my oath, and I will remember it true." Pell reads the page twice and answers all three at once. "The chirurgery gets a warden at the door tonight, and the scribe moves to guest quarters under the House's eye. You may question the carrier with a warden present; he is ours to keep until the House says otherwise. And yes, when she wakes, she wakes to you, not to me." The collector, seated in the side room, unbound, a warden at the door, answers like a man emptying pockets he does not mind turning out. (No rolls: he gives what costs him nothing and stops where his bond begins.) The solicitor: "I have never seen the patron, and neither has my house. The order comes through a brassplate solicitor in the Clerk's Ward. The seal is an open eye, the office is on Seer's Row, up the stair over a mapmaker's. The coin is always old, always exact." The finders are scouts, street people paid a finder's fee per head for the ones nobody will ask after. "I never learn their names; the solicitor pays them, not me." The spending: "I deliver them breathing. What is spent, and how, happens after my contract ends." There he stops, flat and final. The patron's name he does not know, and the bond's terms he will not break. "My house will answer this detention with coin and paper, Herald Brother. Nothing about tonight required blood." In the chirurgery the woman wakes by degrees with Kestrel's face the first thing she sees, a kind face and a steady voice: safe, House of Dust, no chute tonight. Her name is Bria, a laundry-worker from the Hive with no family that asks after her. What she remembers comes in shards and stops where it hurts: a printed notice, work for those who want to leave a life behind; a kind voice that said her debts could be lifted; a cup of something sweet. Then the cart. She presses her knuckles to her eyes. "My mother's face. I can't. It was there yesterday. Why can't I see my mother's face?" The spending, it seems, does not wait for the sale to finish.

Round 19. Nib sits on the other side of Bria's litter from Kestrel, small and gentle, not crowding her. "Bria, my name is Nib. I woke up without most of my memory too, a few days ago, in this same house. I will not question you. But some of it does come back, in pieces. I remembered writing a letter only this evening, and it came whole, like a lamp being lit. May I do one small thing, with your leave? I can cast a quiet spell, no pain, no touch, that shows only whether magic is laid on a person and what kind. It might tell us whether the forgetting was a drug or something else, and how to mend it. If you would rather I did not, I will not." Bria looks from Kestrel's steady face to the small gnome asking her leave, and nods once, gripping the blanket. Nib casts Detect Magic as a ritual, no slot, and the world comes into the spell's focus. Bria glimmers. A faint aura lies on her, thin as breath on glass: Enchantment, and it is not still. It moves the way ink moves in water, slow threads drawing off her and going somewhere he cannot see. The forgetting is not the drug; the drug only kept her quiet for the road. This is a working, and it is still working. And because of the spell's reach he sees the rest: Kestrel, kneeling at the litter, carries the same aura, long spent, a watermark, a stain where ink once was. When he turns the focus on himself, it is there too. All three of them were spent the same way. What is happening to Bria now happened to them, and finished. He ends the ritual gently and tells them all of it, plainly. The chirurgeon, asked about the drug, names a press-ganger's syrup, sweet and cheap, "sailor's mercy" on the docks. It sedates, it does not erase. The two men will sleep it off by morning. She has no physic for the other thing and has never seen its like. A drug can be slept off. A working like this has to be stopped, or what was taken has to be got back, and the spending of Bria is still happening, thread by slow thread, while they stand there.

Round 20. Nib closes his eyes a moment and steadies himself. "I am sorry. It is a working, and it is still taking from you, but it is going somewhere, and what goes somewhere can be followed." With Bria's leave he casts Detect Magic again as a ritual and studies the threads. (Arcana 13 + 6 = 19 vs DC 14: success.) Each thread drifts from Bria the way smoke drifts from a banked coal, slow enough that one takes long minutes to cross his sight, steady as a siphon. Whatever was built to do this was built to run, not to finish. They do not thin as they go. Spell-leak frays; this does not. The threads hold together to the edge of the spell's reach and are gone, so they are not leaking. They are being received. Every one of them leans the same way, rising off her and bending up-street, out of the Hive, toward the Clerk's Ward side of the city. He fixes the line against the doorframe and the window-bar, a bearing to a street, not a door. Pell produces what the House has, a grease-marked collection-rounds map of the Hive, streets drawn for corpse-carts and not scholars. The bearing laid across it runs out of the Hive's warren toward the Clerk's Ward border, and the direction of Seer's Row is consistent with it, no more. Sigil's bent streets laugh at straight lines. Then Kestrel kneels and lays her open hand on Bria's shoulder and prays. "Hold. Whatever is reaching for you, it does not take you while I am here." The ward settles, the way a door closing settles a drafty room: Protection from Evil and Good, whole and true, ten minutes of it. Nib, still inside his ritual, watches the threads. They do not ease. They do not so much as tremble. The ward is a fence built for wolves, and this is wind. Whatever is drawing on Bria is not the touch of an aberration, or a fey, or a fiend, or any dead thing, because the prayer closes every one of those doors and the working walks through none of them. It simply continues, thread by patient thread. That is a list of what it is not. Bria knows only that a knight is kneeling beside her and a small gnome is watching over her, and for a little while she is not being brave alone. Her grip on the blanket loosens.

DM Ruling. The engine blocked Kestrel's cast ("armor you are not proficient with") because the platform sheet stores an empty armor-proficiency list. The standing table ruling is that her approved paladin training (all armor, shields) governs, and the cast proceeds. Kestrel has 2 of 3 first-level slots left. What the party learned: direction (toward the Clerk's Ward side, consistent with Seer's Row but not precise), pace (slow, built to run), delivery (received, not leaked), and one elimination (not the influence of the listed creature types). What they did not learn: destination, mechanism, or whether it can be stopped or undone.

Round 21. Nib ends his ritual and thanks Kestrel for her prayer and for staying with Bria. "It is not a leak. The threads are being received, slowly and steadily, from the Hive toward the Clerk's Ward side. That fits Seer's Row, but no more than fits. It also is not any creature that Kestrel's ward closes against. So it is a working, made to run, and we have to find who is receiving it." He asks Pell for two wardens beside Bria and whether the House knows the open-eye solicitor or the mapmaker whose stair leads up to the office. "We would go to Seer's Row as customers at the mapmaker's shop, quietly, and not break any door." Kestrel, still kneeling, answers. "Yes. When the ward has run its course and she is sleeping safe, we go to Seer's Row as customers, quietly, and break no doors. I will walk beside you and keep rear watch." Mara studies Pell's map with Nib. "I'll look at the stair. First I want a way back down that doesn't depend on the same door." She asks Pell to point out the shop and any public passage or second way out nearby, and proposes entering as a customer to look over the stair, its sightlines and any visible guards. "If somebody's watching it, I'll come back and tell you what I saw." Pell grants two wardens for the chirurgery with a nod and sends a runner. The open-eye seal he knows, the way a records-man knows seals. "A brassplate house on Seer's Row. Their mark is an open eye over a ledger. The Row calls the house the Open Eye, because no one uses the name on the plate. Quiet money. Bonded, bonded deep. They move coin for clients who pay extra to be FORGOTTEN by the paperwork." A dry Dustman pause. "The House does not deal with them. The House forgets for free, in the end." The plate hangs above a mapmaker's shopfront, exactly as Nib supposed, and the solicitor's stair rises from within. Pell taps the narrow mews lane behind the shop where delivery carts go, a second way in and out of most brassplate houses. While Kestrel's ward stands she also opens her Divine Sense, and the room comes to her like a chord to a tuned ear. No celestial. No fiend. Nothing wearing a mortal face in the room is anything but mortal. Undeath is everywhere in this House, patient zombie labor in the corridors and workrooms beyond sixty feet of stone, exactly where the Dustmen keep it, but none of it is here and none of it is bent on the woman on the litter. Whatever is spending Bria is not standing in this room in any shape her oath knows. The ward's last minute runs out like sand, and Bria's breathing deepens into true sleep. They have a plate, a street, a shop, a stair and a lane behind it, and for the first time since the chute, all three are awake, whole enough and free to choose the next hour together.

DM Ruling. In the 2024 rules Divine Sense is a Channel Divinity effect, and paladins at this level have two uses (one back on a short rest, all on a long). The earlier note of 1/1 was wrong; Kestrel had 2/2 and now has 1/2. Her sense answers whether something of those kinds is here, not who or what stands behind the working from afar. The mapmaker's shop under the solicitor's stair is adopted from Nib's plan and is canon. Pell's mews-lane detail is a lead to verify on site, not a guarantee.

Seer's Row

Scribes' stalls stand open and advocates argue fees in doorways; the air smells of ink and sealing-wax instead of rendering-fat. Seer's Row is a narrow terrace of brass plates, every door a professional discreetness.

Round 22. Nib looks once more at Bria sleeping, thanks Pell and the chirurgeon, and says to the others, "We have a plate, a street, a shop, a stair and a lane behind it. Let us go, quietly, now, while she is sleeping and the working is not finished with her." To Pell: "Brother Pell, if we have not come back by the next bell, would you send one warden to Seer's Row to ask after three customers of the mapmaker's? And please keep the copy of that page safe." He proposes the order of march: Mara first, by whatever way she judges best, to look at the mews lane and any other way in or out, while he walks in by the front as a scholar wanting a map of the Clerk's Ward, Kestrel as his escort. Kestrel rises from her knees, looks once at the sleeping woman to be sure her breathing is deep and even, and answers. "Yes. She sleeps, and the wardens have her; my watch here is done for now. I will walk beside you as your escort and keep rear watch, and I will ask after no stair and no solicitor. But no one goes up that stair until Mara has had her look and told us what she saw."
