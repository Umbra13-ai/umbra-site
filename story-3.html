The Open Eye

The stair climbs one flight of worn stone and turns once. Beyond the door is a receiving room the way a counting-house is a shop: low lamplight, bare polished wood, chairs that no one has ever sat in twice. Nothing is written anywhere the eye can find; the room feels swept not of dust but of record.

Round 46. At the foot of the stair Kestrel turns from the door to face the other three and reports plainly. "My sense found nothing in the open street — no celestial, no fiend, no undead — and nothing on any of us beyond what we openly carry. But the house stands behind stone and shut wood, and my sense does not pass through it. It rules nothing out." She nods to Nib. "I take your word, papers — if any of us says it, we turn and go down together, no argument. No names, no papers, no gifts, no cups, and if any of us feels our own name slip or a memory go, we say the word." Then she meets Ashen's eye. "My word is to climb, as four — and I will lead the moment you speak yours. Until then I hold here, at the foot of the stair." She stays planted there, shield on her arm, eyes moving between the door, the tray and the cart men lingering on the Row. Ashen meets her eye, then nods once to Mara and Nib. "My word is with yours. We climb as four — no names, no papers, no gifts, no cups. If any of us says papers, we turn and go down together, no argument. If a name slips or a memory goes, same word." She glances at the tray and the door, then takes her place at the rear as Mara set it. "Kestrel leads. I'll watch our backs and the Row behind us. Ready when you are." Her hands stay empty of anything that could be laid on the tray, and with her word, there are four. Kestrel takes the first step. The stair climbs one flight of worn stone and turns once, and below, the Row goes on pretending not to watch. The fifth bell begins to ring as Kestrel reaches the landing, and before her knuckles find the door, it opens. A person stands in the gap, neither young nor old, dressed like a clerk of some unremarkable house, in gray that has never once been noticed. Their gaze moves over the four and pauses, just perceptibly, on the count. "The house was told to expect the party of the unnamed," the clerk says, voice like a page turning. "It finds four where the approach spoke of none at all." A small pause, not unfriendly. "The Eye will decide what that is worth. You bring nothing written — the tray below stands empty, and the house is glad of it. Come as you are, then. You already have." They step aside, and a second door stands shut at the far end of the room, with lamplight breathing at its seam. The clerk folds their hands. "You may speak freely here. Nothing said in this room is written down. Nothing written down may leave it." The smallest smile. "Those are the same rule, twice." The clerk waits, and by the house's own terms, the floor is theirs.

Round 47. Kestrel steps a pace forward, shield on her arm, and meets the clerk's gaze, speaking first as the terms ask. "Then I speak first, as your terms ask. We are four who woke with our pasts cut out of us, and we have since found others cut the same way — the living, taken breathing and left spent of memory. We came to learn who takes them, and why, and what the Eye knows of it. We bring nothing written, no names, no gifts, no cups, and we sign nothing. Ask what you will of us, and we will answer or we will not — and any one of us may end this and walk out, as your own terms allow." She holds her ground at the front of the four, hands open at her sides. Mara lets her finish, then looks to the clerk. "Before we go through another door, I want one part of your rule plain. If we remember what we hear here and walk out with those memories, are we free to do that? We came to recover what was taken from us. I won't agree to leave more of it behind without knowing." The clerk hears Kestrel through without blinking, head tilting at each clause like a man tallying a clean column, and answers Mara's question first, plainly. "What you remember is yours. The house's rule concerns the pen, not the skull. Callers walk out with everything they carried in, and with whatever they came to buy — that is rather the point of a house that sells forgetting." A pause, the smallest smile again. "The house has never needed to hold anyone." Then, to Kestrel, and through her to all four: "What the Eye knows, it sells. What it sells, it prices. The question was never whether the Eye knows."

The Inner Room

Lamplight the color of late honey fills the room. The shelves hold nothing at all, and a wide desk stands with its top bare as a fresh page. Someone is seated behind it, hands folded, waiting the way still water waits.

Round 48. The clerk turns, the inner door opens at their touch, and they stand aside. "The Eye will see you now." Mara gives the clerk a small nod and steps through, keeping her companions in sight, and faces the person behind the bare desk. "Then let's begin with the price. Who is taking the people we found, and where can we reach them? What would those two answers cost?" She waits to hear the offer before agreeing to any exchange. Nib steps through close beside her, his face as empty as the bare desk, and leaves the asking to her. With a single small gesture that a scholar might make to straighten his sleeve, he casts Detect Magic the ordinary way to see whether the seated figure, the desk, the lamp or the empty shelves carry any magic, and at the same time he reads the figure for what they feel. He says nothing aloud, gives no name, touches nothing, and keeps Shield ready and the word papers on his tongue. (Nib spends a first-level slot. Insight 10 + 1 = 11 vs DC 13: failure.) The small gesture completes itself against his sleeve, and the seated figure's eyes find him at once, not startled and not angered. Pleased, if anything, the way a pawnbroker is pleased by a customer who checks the scales. "A small working, caller," the figure says, voice quiet and unhurried, and the lamp seems to lean into it. "The house does not take offense. It takes note." The spell shows Nib that the lamp carries a fine divination thread, twin to the tray's below. The bare desk and the empty shelves carry nothing at all, and that nothing feels deliberate. The seated figure, though, carries enchantment, in layers, deep and old and carefully kept, and the make of it is the same spent-watermark signature, stronger here than anywhere he has yet stood. What it does, the spell cannot say, and his own Mage Armor hums familiar at the edge of it all. The figure is composed and unhurried, attention moving over all four callers the way a clerk's fingers move over coins, weighing and not fearing. Beneath the stillness there is an appetite, but it is not cruelty and it is not kindness. It is interest. The folded hands open, palms up, a merchant's invitation. "Two answers asked: who takes the breathing and leaves them spent, and where that one can be reached." The figure inclines their head to Mara. "You have already stood where they count. That much the house gives you free, because you earned the standing. For the rest, the Eye prices in what is unwritten. One living memory of the working, given freely by one who carries it. Given whole, and given up. The one who sells remembers that they sold, and what they bought, and nothing of the working itself. The house forgets nothing." A pause, courteous as a knife laid flat. "Or coin, if you prefer the cruder scale. The name and the reach together: four hundred pieces of gold." The figure's hands fold again. "Take the room's time. Words are cheap here, and you may keep yours."

Round 49. Kestrel holds her ground at the front of the four, shield on her arm, and meets the seated figure's gaze. "Then hear my answer plainly. We will not pay in memory. We came here because memories are being cut out of the living, and I will not hand the Eye one of my own freely — not mine, and not one of my companions'. That is the very theft we mean to end. As for coin — we do not carry four hundred gold between the four of us, and I will not pretend otherwise. So tell me what else the Eye takes. A deed? A lesser answer — the name alone, or the reach alone? Or a price we can carry? We are four, and we decide together; no one of us sells alone. Ask what you will, and we will answer or we will not — but the price you named, we do not pay." She waits, hands open at her sides. The figure is still for a moment, and in the stillness the lamp-flame stands straight as a pin. "A principled refusal," the Eye says at last. "The house hears those so rarely it almost remembers them." Something that is not quite a laugh moves through the quiet. "And an honest account of an empty purse. You deal cleaner than most who climb this stair, caller. The house takes note — again." The folded hands tip, weighing. "A lesser answer, then. The name alone: one hundred and fifty in gold. The reach alone — where the counter can be found — three hundred. You will observe that knowing who is cheap, and knowing where is not. The house did not make that true; it only prices it. Or a deed, as you offer. Here is the one the Eye would take." The voice stays level, courteous, patient. "You mean to end this working — you have said so, standing in the Eye's own room. Very well. The house will give you the name and the reach tonight, against a price you carry already: when the working is undone, you return to this stair and give the Eye the true account of its unmaking. Told, not written — the house keeps its own rule. Who made it, how it held, and how it broke. Whole and true, as you lived it." The gaze moves across the four of them, unhurried, weighing each. "You would be selling a story you do not yet own. The house buys it in advance, because the house forgets nothing, and because a working that unmakes memory is the rarest stock that has walked through that door in years. Consider what it means, callers: the Eye would know everything you learn. That is the price. Choose together, as you say you do. The room's time is still yours."

Round 50. Mara looks from the bare desk to the others, then back to the Eye. "An account of how we ended it, I could offer for myself. Instructions for making another one, I won't sell. Would you take the story with the survivors' identities and anything that would let someone copy the working left out?" She glances toward her companions. "That's the bargain I'd consider." The Eye's head tilts, and for a long breath the room is only lamplight. "You would sell the breaking and keep the making." A slow nod, almost approving. "Caller, the house will tell you a secret it sells to no one: a working that can be copied is worth more broken than bought. The Eye does not deal in stock every cutter can mint. Keep your making. The house prefers to be the only house that knows what it knows. And your survivors' names — keep those as well. The house has never needed help finding people." The smallest pause. "It finds them." The folded hands open one finger at a time. "So the bargain shapes itself: the name and the reach, tonight. Against it, when the working is undone, the true account of its unmaking — whole in the breaking. What held it, what broke it, and whose hand struck. Told to this room, not written. The rest — your friends, your methods of the making — stays yours, unasked. But hear the house's shape clearly: one voice offers, and four must carry it. The account will be owed by the four of you together, whoever of you lives to tell it. If any one of you will not be bound, say so now, and the house will sell you something smaller instead." The Eye settles back, patient as a ledger. "Speak among yourselves. The room's time remains yours."

Round 51. Nib steps half a pace forward beside Mara, hands visible and empty, and speaks for himself, mildly. "I thank the house for naming a price we can talk about, and for keeping the survivors out of it. Before the four of us answer, I would ask three things, and then I will say plainly where I stand. First, the name and the reach: do they lead to whoever receives what is being drawn from the people, the patron, or only to a hand that works for them? Second, you say one voice offers and four must carry it. Carry it how? Is the bond a spoken word, or is it laid on us by something that cannot be broken, and what happens to the three if one of us is gone before the account is told? Third, the account, what held it, what broke it and whose hand struck: is it owed only once the working is truly undone, and only to this room, with nothing kept or written?" He listens, then adds, "For myself, I will give my word to a plain spoken account without the methods of the making, if the bond is only my word. I will not be bound by any magic I did not see made. If that is the house's shape, tell me what binds, and if it is too much, I will take the smaller thing." He says nothing of the names of anyone he knows, signs nothing, and keeps the word papers ready. Ashen stays a half-step back from the bare desk, hands empty and visible, and glances first to Mara, then Nib, then Kestrel before she looks at the seated figure. "I hear the shape," she says, quiet and even. "Name and reach tonight, against a true spoken account of the unmaking when it is done — what held it, what broke it, whose hand struck — told in this room, not written. Making and survivors left unasked. That much I can carry, for myself." She tips her chin toward Nib. "But I stand with his three questions before any of us says yes. Who the name and reach actually lead to. What 'four must carry' means — spoken word only, or something laid on us that we cannot break, and what falls on the rest if one of us is gone. And that the account is owed only once the working is truly undone, only here, with nothing kept or written outside this room." To the Eye, then to her companions: "For myself: if the bond is only my word — no signature, no cup, no gift, no working I did not see made — I will give that word to the limited account Mara named. I will not sell a memory of the making, and I will not be bound by magic I cannot see. If any one of the four will not carry it, or if the answers break those terms, I take the smaller thing instead, or we walk. Papers still ends it for me." The Eye receives the three questions the way a scale receives weights, one at a time and without hurry. "The first: what the name and the reach buy you. The name is the one who counts. The reach is where the counting is done. Whether the hand at that ledger is the heart of the business or a finger of it — the Eye sells what it can warrant, and its warranty reaches the counter, not past it. If there is a hand above that hand, the house does not sell what it cannot swear to. You are buying the counter, callers. The house says so plainly. The second: what binds. Your spoken word, given in this room, in the Eye's hearing. Nothing laid on you — no working, no mark, no cup, no cord. The house does not need them. The house forgets nothing, and the Row knows its friends; a debt remembered is a debt paid, in time. That is the whole of the binding." The folded hands spread, empty. "And if one of you is gone before the telling: the account is owed by the four, whoever lives to tell it. If only one voice climbs back down that stair, one voice pays it whole. The third: yes. Owed only when the working is truly undone — the house is patient; it knows a debt ripening. Owed only to this room. Nothing written, nothing carried out but the hearing of it. The house keeps its own rule even in its own house." The figure settles back into the lamplight, hands folding once more. "Those are the answers, and the shape stands as your friend named it: the breaking whole, the making and the survivors unasked. Four words are owed before the goods change hands. The Eye has all the time the bells allow."

Round 52. Kestrel stands steady at the front of the four, shield on her arm, hands open, and speaks to the Eye for herself only. "I heard your answers, and I answer plainly — for myself only. I do not speak for the others. The name you sell reaches the counter and no further. I take it as named, with open eyes. A counter is still a place to begin. My word, and only my word: when the working is truly undone, I will stand in this room and tell you true what held it, what broke it, and whose hand struck — whole in the breaking, spoken and not written, heard and not carried out. The making stays mine, and the survivors stay unasked. I heard what you said about finding people, and I hold you to your own shape on that. The bond is my spoken word, given here in your hearing. No working, no mark, no cup, no cord — nothing laid on me I did not see made. And I have heard the weight of it: if only one of us climbs back down that stair, that one pays the account whole. I carry that knowingly. A true telling is a burden I can bear. That is my word. It is given." Mara nods once. "That's a promise I can keep. When the working is truly undone, I'll return to this room and tell you what held it, what broke it, and whose hand struck. Whole in the breaking, with the making and the survivors left unasked, as you've agreed. If I'm the one left to tell it, I'll carry the telling." She meets the Eye's gaze. "For myself, you have my word." Four words are given one after another in the lamplit quiet, and the Eye listens to each as if it were being read a page it would one day recite. When the last of them lands, the figure inclines its head. "Given, and heard, and kept. The house does not write it down." The smallest pause. "The house is the writing down. Then the goods, callers, as warranted — the counter, and no further. The name you bought is Tally. Not the house's name borrowed for a signboard — the blood's own. The book is kept by Mordecai Tally, called the Tallyman where he cannot hear it, and not called anything at all where he can. It is his voice that counts coin behind the curtain. It is his book that holds wages against new names. And it is his counting that the taken are spent against, a line at a time, until the line runs out." The Eye's voice does not change as it goes on. "The reach: you have stood in his front room. The Gilded Tally counts in the front, where the Row can watch it count — that is the shop's respectability, and its camouflage. The book itself is kept below. Behind the curtain at the back of the front room there is a stair, and at the foot of it the under-count, where the counting is done that daylight must not see. He counts at night, when the mercy flows back from Kettle's door and the day's takings come home." The figure's hands fold a final time. "The house does not sell advice, but it gives away what costs it nothing: go when the book is open, or do not go at all. And the house will tell you one thing free, because it amuses the house to say it aloud — you are not the first to buy this reach. You are the first to buy it with your eyes open. The account is owed when the working is undone. The Eye will be here. It is always here." The lamplight dims, courteous as a bow, and the gray clerk appears soundless at the inner door and holds it wide. Down one flight of worn stone the Row receives them back, with lamplight, evening air, and the smell of rain somewhere far off. The door closes without a sound behind them. As the four reach the foot of the stair the sixth bell begins to ring across the Ward, and somewhere down the Row a cart man scratches his ear, and the wardens who were waiting turn and walk away, unneeded. They carry nothing written. They carry a name, and a stair, and a debt. (Milestone, the Open Eye parley: 250 XP each to Nib, Kestrel, Mara and Ashen.)

Pell's Back Room

The sixth bell fades behind you as evening takes the streets. The day-shift doss-men queue with their bowls, the lamplighters stand halfway up their ladders, and the air smells of lye and rain that has not yet fallen. In the back room, the map-table and the lamp turned low.

Round 53. At the foot of the stair, under the sixth bell, Nib lets out a long breath and speaks quietly to the other three. "Mordecai Tally, and a book kept below the front room, counted at night. We carry no paper and we carry a debt we agreed to. The Eye said go when the book is open, or not at all. I would not go in as four strangers with a cart man's watch behind us. The House has its own grievance: the Tally stamped Brother Pell's ledger to bill the living as dead. Let us go back to Pell now, tell him the name, and ask whether the House will come with us, with wardens and its own right to look at that book, and let the four of us go in beside him, not ahead of him." He proposes a return to the House first to look in on Bria and eat, and a street watch of the Gilded Tally's front and the Kettle Wheel's takings route at dusk, with Kestrel to lead the talk with Pell as the one he trusts. He asks Ashen to stay with them and keep to the terms, and says he will not go down any stair behind the curtain without Pell or the wardens, and will say papers if anyone is cut off from the rest. Kestrel turns the party toward the Mortuary and speaks plainly. "Nib is right. We carry a name and a stair and a debt — and the House has its own grievance, the Tally stamping Brother Pell's ledger to bill the living as dead. We go to Pell now, all four of us, and I will do the talking — he trusts me, and he should hear the name from us before anyone else counts on it. Mara, watch the front as you judge best; Nib, the dusk watch is yours to call. And on the climb itself, my word stands: Ashen takes the outside watch when we go down. She has said she will take either, and I would rather have her eyes on the Row than a fourth blade in a stairwell we cannot see. I will not go down behind that curtain without Pell or the wardens beside us — not tonight, not on a stranger's say-so." She takes the rear as they walk, shield on her arm. The four leave Seer's Row for the long walk down into the Hive, and evening has the streets by the time the Mortuary's gray walls rise. The wardens at the gate know their faces now and wave them through without a word. Inside, Bria sleeps as she has slept, two wardens at her door, her breathing slow and even, unchanged, which tonight counts as mercy. The two rescued men are sitting up over stew in the side room, and one of them raises a hand as the party passes, a small gesture from a man who a day ago could not remember his own name. Brother Pell is where he always is, in the back room by the map-table, the lamp turned low. He looks up as the four of them fill his doorway, and whatever he reads in their faces makes him set his pen down flat across the page, a man closing a ledger he will not need. "You've come back with something," Pell says quietly. "I can see it costing you the keeping of it. Sit, or stand, as you like — but say it plain. I've had my fill of pretty words this week." He waits, hands folded on the map of the Row, and the lamp gutters once, and is still.

Round 54. Mara stands at the edge of the map-table. "The counter is Mordecai Tally. The book is below the Gilded Tally's front room, down a stair behind the back curtain. He counts at night, when the takings come back from Kettle's door." She taps the table beside the map, without marking it. "Could your wardens claim the right to inspect that book? I can watch the front from the street and look for the takings coming home, but I'd rather know who's coming through the door with us before we go below." Pell does not move when the name lands. For a long moment he only looks at the map under his hands, and when he speaks his voice is very level, the way men speak when they are holding something heavy. "Mordecai Tally. The Gilded Tally is older than my office, and respectable the way a knife is respectable. And it was his stamp — his house's stamp — on my ledger, billing breathing men as dead. I've carried that page for weeks, not knowing who to hang it on." He straightens. "Now I know." He turns the question over like a surveyor. "Can my wardens claim the right to look at his book? Yes — on paper, and this once I mean that kindly. Where the dead are lied about, the House has the right to count: his false billing gives me cause, and cause gives me a seal, and a seal opens his front room and his ledger to my eyes, with wardens at my back. That much the law of the Ward gives me plainly." A finger taps the map, once, on the curtain that isn't drawn there. "What the seal does not name is what lies below. His under-count is his own cellar, and no writ I can draw this week says the word cellar. But hear me: if we stand in his front room on lawful cause, and the fraud shows itself — and it will — then what we find behind that curtain is evidence of the same crime, and no magistrate will ask which side of the curtain we read it on. You go down as citizens assisting my inspection. That is thin, but it is honest thin, and it is the best the law will carry." He looks at each of them in turn. "Two things more. I need a day — a seal drawn properly, and wardens I trust with something this size. Not Kettle's gossips, not the new men. Two, maybe three. We can go tonight on your say-so alone, thin as paper, and I will come if you ask it. But I would rather go tomorrow night strong than tonight loud. And the last thing, and keep it close: if his book holds wages against new names, then whatever name he wrote YOU down as is in that book. If he learns you came asking before you come counting, the book will be shut when you arrive. So watch his front from the street, Mara of the patient feet — but watch it like rain, not like weather." He picks his pen back up, and sets it down again without dipping it. "Tell me: tonight thin, or tomorrow night with a seal and three wardens. Either way, the House comes with you. You brought me the name; the least of my debts is the going."

Round 55. Ashen stays a half-step back from the map-table, hands empty, and meets Pell's look when he asks the room. "Tomorrow, with the seal," she says, quiet enough that the hallway need not hear it. "Tonight is paper. The Eye said go when the book is open — not when we are loud and thin. If his book already holds wages against names that might be ours, asking before counting closes it. Better three wardens you trust and a writ that opens the front than four strangers kicking a curtain tonight." She glances to Mara, then Nib and Kestrel. "When we go down, I take the outside watch, as Kestrel set — eyes on the Row, not a fourth blade in a stair we cannot see. Until then I can walk the front with Mara like rain, not weather: lamps, carts, whether the takings look like they came home. I will not go behind that curtain without you or your wardens beside us." She tips her chin once to Pell. "House comes with us either way. We brought you the name; we wait the day if that is what makes the going strong." Nib hears Pell out and answers first with thanks. "Tomorrow night, with the seal and the wardens you trust. Thin is honest, but I would rather arrive strong than loud. Brother Pell, may I ask three things for the day? The Eye's rule about paper held only for its own stair, so may I have my spellbook back, sealed or not, to prepare my spells properly after tonight's rest? The under-count may be written in a cipher, and I can read most hands with the right spell if I have the book. Second, does the House have anyone, an under-cantor or an archivist, who knows anything of workings that take memory, who might slow what is being done to Bria while we wait? Third, I will not go near the Gilded Tally's front, since its clerks have seen me at the counter; Mara and Ashen should watch it, like rain, and only if they judge it safe." He proposes that they look in on Bria and the two men, eat, take a proper long rest in the House so that Kestrel's Lay on Hands and his own slots return, and spend tomorrow quietly with Pell on the seal and the wardens, not on the street. Pell listens to Ashen and then to Nib and nods once, the matter settled in his head. "Tomorrow night, then. Strong, not loud. The seal takes me a day regardless — let it take a day for something." He rises, goes to the strongbox in the corner, works the key, and comes back with the sealed parcel the party left in his keeping. He breaks the seal in front of them, deliberately, letting them watch it break, and lays the contents on the map-table: the map, the handbill, the page, the Tally certificate, and Nib's spellbook, untouched. "The Eye's rule held on its own stair," Pell says. "It has no writ in my house. Your paper is your own. Prepare what you need, wizard — if his book is ciphered, I want a reader in the room who can crack it." To the second question his face does something careful. "There is Sister Ansa, under-cantor of the closed stacks. She keeps the cases nobody is proud of. I sent for her before you came — your two men downstairs are not the first the House has seen spent this way. The old records call it debts forgiven and forgot, and it surfaces every few years, always near money, always near a door that pours something sweet. What she knows that is worth your candles: the ones who sleep do not spend. Whatever it draws, it draws slower from the still ones. Bria sleeping is not Bria lost. She will sit with the girl tomorrow, and bring what the stacks hold on workings that take memory — what little it is." He straightens the map where the parcel bent it. "And the third is only wisdom. Stay off his street, Nib. His clerks know your face at the counter. The House keeps bunks for guests of the office. Eat, rest — a true rest, behind wardens, under a roof that answers to me. Tomorrow I draw the seal and choose my wardens. Mara and Ashen watch his front like rain, if they judge it safe. And tomorrow night, when the book opens..." He does not finish it. He blows out the second lamp instead, which is answer enough. The House settles around them with crockery, low voices, and the doss-men's coughing two rooms over. A pallet room with four bunks waits at the end of the hall, and a warden will sit outside it all night.

Round 56. Kestrel meets Pell's look at the map-table and speaks plainly. "Tomorrow night, then — strong, not loud. I will not ask the House to go thin on my account. I thank you for the parcel, and for the roof." She rests a hand on the map where the curtain is not drawn. "I will take the first watch outside our door, so your warden's eyes can rest where they are needed. And before I sleep I will look in on Bria — the sleeping ones do not spend, and I would see her breathing even for myself. Sister Ansa has my thanks already, sight unseen." She turns toward the hall. "Tomorrow I stand where you put me, Brother Pell. And when the book opens, I go down only with your wardens at my back." The warden at the pallet room gives her a grateful nod and yields the post without ceremony, because House people know a watch offered is a watch shared. Bria's room is lamplit and quiet. She sleeps as she has slept, breathing slow and even, one hand curled against the blanket like a child holding a place in a book. On the stool beside her someone has set a cup of water and a folded cloth, changed recently, since the House keeps its own kinds of vigil. Watching her, it is not hard to believe Sister Ansa's quiet arithmetic: what sleeps does not spend. She is not losing ground tonight, and tonight, breathing even is enough. Stew and dark bread appear in the side room, and the two rescued men make space at their table without being asked.
